Lesson

1. Dark Surfaces Absorb Heat

The first reason cities become heat islands is simple: dark surfaces absorb more sunlight than light surfaces. City roofs, roads, and parking lots often use dark materials that store heat.

A grass field or forest is different. Plants reflect some sunlight and release water, which cools the air. A dark road cannot do this.

In an Arizona pilot reported by the U.S. EPA, conventional asphalt reached about 67°C (152°F) at midday. The tested cool-pavement surfaces were about 6–9°C (10–16°F) cooler. Those figures describe pavement surface temperature, not the same drop in air temperature.

Lesson

2. Buildings Trap Heat at Night

During the day, concrete and brick buildings absorb heat from the sun. At night, they slowly release this heat back into the air. This is why cities often feel very warm even after sunset.

Tall buildings make the problem worse. In dense areas like central Tokyo or Manhattan in New York, tall buildings form 'urban canyons'. Heat gets trapped between the walls, and wind cannot easily carry it away.

In Japan, a tropical night means the lowest nighttime temperature is 25°C or higher. JMA reported a long-term increase in Tokyo before its observation site moved in 2014. Tokyo's environment bureau also found that areas with many tropical nights had spread west compared with 1992.

Lesson

3. Fewer Trees, Less Cooling

Trees do more than look nice. They give shade, and they release water through their leaves in a process called evapotranspiration. This process cools the surrounding air, similar to how sweat cools human skin.

An EPA review of 308 studies found that urban forests were, on average, 1.6°C cooler than urban areas without green space. This is an average across many studies, not a promise for every street.

NOAA reported that Phoenix, Arizona, rose above 110°F (about 43°C) for 31 days in a row from June 30 through July 30, 2023. Phoenix's Cool Corridors plan uses trees, built shade, and green rainwater features along walking routes, with a 100-mile goal by 2030.

Lesson

4. Human Activity Adds Extra Heat

Cities also produce their own heat. Cars, buses, factories, and air conditioners all release heat outside. This is called anthropogenic heat, which means 'heat made by humans'.

Air conditioning is a difficult example. Inside a building, it cools the room, but outside it releases waste heat. A model checked against observations in two Tokyo office areas estimated that weekday air-conditioner waste heat raised outdoor air temperature by 1–2°C or more. This is a result for those modeled office districts, not a citywide average.

This can create a hard cycle: the city is hot, so people use more air conditioning; the machines release more heat outside; then even more cooling is needed.

Lesson

5. Solutions and Their Tradeoffs

Cities are testing several solutions. Cool roofs use light paint or reflective material to send sunlight back into the sky. By April 2019, NYC CoolRoofs had coated more than 10 million square feet of rooftops since September 2009. The tradeoff is that white roofs may lose some benefit in winter, when a warmer roof would help save heating energy.

Green roofs cover buildings with plants. They can cool the roof and add green space in a dense area. The tradeoff is cost: green roofs are heavier, need water, and are more expensive to build and maintain.

Medellín in Colombia has a program of 30 green corridors along road edges and waterways. The city associates the program with local cooling of up to 2°C. The tradeoff is time and care: trees need years to grow and regular maintenance.

There is no single perfect answer. Each city must choose a mix of solutions that fits its climate, budget, and space.

What you can do now

Summary

Cities become heat islands because several causes work together, including dark surfaces, dense buildings, fewer trees, and human-made heat.

Arizona cool-pavement tests measured lower surface temperatures; they did not show the same drop in air temperature.

Official evidence includes Tokyo's tropical-night trend, Phoenix's 31-day heat record and Cool Corridors plan, and a Tokyo office-district air-conditioning model.

Trees cool through shade and evapotranspiration; an EPA review found urban forests averaged 1.6°C cooler than non-green urban areas.

By April 2019, NYC CoolRoofs had coated more than 10 million square feet, while Medellín's 30-corridor program was linked by the city to local cooling of up to 2°C.

Every solution has tradeoffs in cost, time, maintenance, space, or seasonal effect, so cities need a mix of answers.

Cities can stay warmer than nearby rural areas, especially after sunset. This is the urban heat island effect. Dark surfaces, fewer trees, dense buildings, vehicles, and air conditioners can all add or hold heat.
