A2: Coffee Knowledge 3: Blends, Origins, and Roasts

Quality checked lesson

Coffee bags and café menus often use words such as blend, single origin, light roast, and dark roast. You will learn what these words mean and how to explain a coffee to a customer in clear English.

Before you begin

Warm-Up Questions

  1. What information do you notice on a coffee bag?

  2. Do you like chocolate, nut, fruit, or flower flavours?

  3. What makes a coffee easy to drink with milk?

Reading

1. What Is a Blend?

A blend combines two or more coffees. The coffees may come from different farms, regions, countries, plant varieties, or processing methods. A roaster chooses the parts carefully to make one planned flavour. Blends can help a café offer a stable house coffee through the year. If one seasonal coffee changes, the roaster can adjust another part of the blend. Many espresso blends are also designed to keep a clear coffee taste when milk is added. They may have familiar chocolate, caramel, or nut flavours that work well in a flat white or latte.

Check your understanding

1. Why do cafés often use blends for milk drinks?

Answer

Roasters can design blends to keep a clear, balanced coffee flavour when milk is added.

Look back at this section and find the main reason.

Helpful clue: I would choose __ and __ flavours. These flavours may work well with milk.

Think one step further

  • What flavours would you choose for a house blend?

Reading

2. What Is Single Origin?

Single origin means the coffee comes from one defined origin. The origin can be a country, a region, a cooperative, one farm, or even one small lot. The label should tell the customer how specific the origin is. Cafés often serve single-origin coffee when they want people to notice a special flavour. One coffee may remind people of berries or citrus. Another may have chocolate, nuts, or flowers. These words describe flavour memories. They do not usually mean that berries, chocolate, or flowers were added to the beans.

Check your understanding

1. Does single origin always mean one farm?

Answer

No. It can mean one country, region, cooperative, farm, or individual lot.

Look back at this section and find the main reason.

Helpful clue: I would like to try a coffee with __ notes. It sounds __.

Think one step further

  • Which flavour note would make you curious about a coffee?

Reading

3. Light and Dark Roasts

Roasting changes green coffee into brown, aromatic coffee. A light roast usually keeps more of the coffee's original fruit, flower, and bright character. It may also feel lighter in the mouth. A dark roast develops more roasted flavours. It may taste more bitter, smoky, or like dark chocolate. Light does not always mean weak, and dark does not always mean stronger caffeine. Roast level mainly tells us how far the roaster developed the beans. The final strength in the cup also depends on the dose, water, recipe, and brewing method.

Check your understanding

1. What flavours are more common in a dark roast?

Answer

Dark roasts more often have roasted, bitter, smoky, or dark-chocolate flavours.

Look back at this section and find the main reason.

Helpful clue: I would choose a __ roast because __. I prefer __ flavours.

Think one step further

  • Would you choose a light or dark roast for your next coffee? Why?

Reading

4. Acidity Is Not Always Sourness

Coffee professionals often use the word acidity for a bright, lively taste. Pleasant acidity can remind people of orange, apple, berries, or sparkling fruit. It can make a coffee feel fresh and interesting. Bad sourness is different. It can taste sharp, aggressive, and unbalanced. A poorly made espresso may taste sour because the water did not take enough flavour from the coffee. However, a fruity light roast can be well made and still have pleasant acidity. A good barista listens carefully before deciding that a customer dislikes all acidity.

Check your understanding

1. What is the difference between pleasant acidity and unpleasant sourness?

Answer

Pleasant acidity is bright and balanced, while unpleasant sourness is sharp and unbalanced.

Look back at this section and find the main reason.

Helpful clue: It has a bright taste like __. It is fruity and balanced, not sharply sour. Would you like something rounder and more chocolate-like?

Think one step further

  • How could you explain acidity without making it sound negative?

Reading

Useful Speaking Patterns

- This is our house blend. - It has __ flavours and works well with milk. - This single origin is lighter and fruitier. - It is bright and balanced, not sharply sour.

Story

A customer usually drinks dark-roasted coffee with milk. Today, the barista offers a house blend and a lighter single origin.

Roleplay

Dialogue Practice Role-play

Read once.

Customer

I usually drink dark-roasted coffee. What is your house coffee like?

Barista

Our house blend has chocolate and caramel flavours. It works well with milk.

Customer

And what is the single origin like?

Barista

It is lighter and fruitier, with a bright orange-like acidity.

Customer

Is it very sour?

Barista

No. It is bright but balanced. The house blend is rounder and less fruity.

Customer

I think I will try the house blend as a flat white today.

Barista

Great choice. I will make that for you now.

Customer

Thanks for explaining it clearly.

Barista

You're welcome. I will call your name when it is ready.

Customer

Great. Thank you.

Barista

Have a good day.

Final Speaking Task

  1. Explain why a café may use a blend and what single origin means.

  2. Compare a light roast and a dark roast.

  3. Recommend one coffee to a customer who wants milk and a strong coffee flavour.

What you can do now

Final Reflection

Coffee labels are useful when they help a person imagine the cup. A good barista gives a short, honest explanation and checks what the customer enjoys. How would you describe your ideal coffee in three simple phrases?
