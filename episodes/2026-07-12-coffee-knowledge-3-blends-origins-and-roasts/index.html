Reading

1. What Is a Blend?

A blend combines two or more coffees.

The coffees may come from different farms, regions, countries, plant varieties, or processing methods.

A roaster chooses the parts carefully to make one planned flavour.

Blends can help a café offer a stable house coffee through the year.

If one seasonal coffee changes, the roaster can adjust another part of the blend.

Many espresso blends are also designed to keep a clear coffee taste when milk is added.

They may have familiar chocolate, caramel, or nut flavours that work well in a flat white or latte.

Reading

2. What Is Single Origin?

Single origin means the coffee comes from one defined origin.

The origin can be a country, a region, a cooperative, one farm, or even one small lot.

The label should tell the customer how specific the origin is.

Cafés often serve single-origin coffee when they want people to notice a special flavour.

One coffee may remind people of berries or citrus.

Another may have chocolate, nuts, or flowers.

These words describe flavour memories.

They do not usually mean that berries, chocolate, or flowers were added to the beans.

Reading

3. Light and Dark Roasts

Roasting changes green coffee into brown, aromatic coffee.

A light roast usually keeps more of the coffee's original fruit, flower, and bright character.

It may also feel lighter in the mouth.

A dark roast develops more roasted flavours.

It may taste more bitter, smoky, or like dark chocolate.

Light does not always mean weak, and dark does not always mean stronger caffeine.

Roast level mainly tells us how far the roaster developed the beans.

The final strength in the cup also depends on the dose, water, recipe, and brewing method.

Reading

4. Acidity Is Not Always Sourness

Coffee professionals often use the word acidity for a bright, lively taste.

Pleasant acidity can remind people of orange, apple, berries, or sparkling fruit.

It can make a coffee feel fresh and interesting.

Bad sourness is different.

It can taste sharp, aggressive, and unbalanced.

A poorly made espresso may taste sour because the water did not take enough flavour from the coffee.

However, a fruity light roast can be well made and still have pleasant acidity.

A good barista listens carefully before deciding that a customer dislikes all acidity.
