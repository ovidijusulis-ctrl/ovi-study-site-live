Reading

Dislike Is Not the Same as Boredom

K. Maja Krakowiak and Mary Beth Oliver compared stories with good, bad, or morally mixed characters.

The bad characters were liked the least.

However, their stories were just as suspenseful and mentally engaging as the others.

This gives us the first answer: liking a character and paying attention to a character are different.

A villain can be unpleasant and still make a story difficult to leave.

Reading

1. Danger Gets Priority

Psychologist Arne Öhman and his team asked people to find one different face in a grid.

Across five experiments, people found threatening faces faster and more accurately than friendly faces.

This was not a study of villains, so it is only one part of the answer.

It shows a useful part of attention: possible danger can get priority.

A fictional villain adds plans and choices, so we keep watching for what that person might do next.

Reading

2. Curiosity Turns Danger Into a Question

Psychologist Coltan Scrivner developed a 24-question Morbid Curiosity Scale.

It measures interest in four areas: dangerous minds, violence between people, injured bodies, and paranormal danger.

In the final experiment, people chose between dark and neutral pictures or descriptions.

People with higher scores were more likely to choose the dark information.

Villains fit one area especially well: the minds of dangerous people.

We want to know the motive, plan, or hidden history.

Reading

3. Safe Fear Can Feel Good

Paul Rozin and his team studied 29 experiences that usually feel unpleasant, including fear, sadness, pain, strong tastes, and physical exhaustion.

Many people said they enjoyed some of them when the experience was safe.

The researchers called this benign masochism.

A frightening story can work in a similar way.

Your body reacts, but your mind knows you can pause the film or close the book.

The danger feels real enough to be exciting, but not real enough to hurt you.

Reading

4. Mixed Motives Make Prediction Hard

Some villains are not simply cruel.

They may love one person, protect a group, or regret one choice while continuing to cause harm.

This creates moral ambiguity: we can understand part of the character without approving of the action.

In Krakowiak and Oliver's study, morally mixed characters were liked less than good characters, yet their stories were equally suspenseful, engaging, and enjoyable.

When several motives are possible, the audience keeps testing predictions: Will the villain change, repeat the harm, or surprise us?

This uncertainty creates suspense.

Reading

Use the Four-Lens Test

Different villains use different combinations.

Darth Vader brings threat, but his past also creates a question about change.

Iago in Othello tells the audience about his plan, so suspense comes from waiting to see who will understand the trap.

Frankenstein's creature causes harm but also describes rejection and loneliness, which creates moral ambiguity.

Fun fact: Mary Shelley never gives the creature a personal name; Frankenstein is the creator.

When a villain stays in your mind, ask four questions: What is the threat?

What information is missing?

Why does the story feel safe?

Which motive is difficult to predict?
