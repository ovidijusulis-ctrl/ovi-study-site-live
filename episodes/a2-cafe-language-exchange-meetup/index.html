  3. Which language would you like to practise with a native speaker?

Reading

Part 1: How a Language Exchange Works

A language exchange is a trade: you give something, and you get something. You help someone with your language, and they help you with theirs. For example, you speak Japanese and want to practise English. Then you look for an English speaker who wants to practise Japanese. Many language cafés split the time in half. You talk in one language for half an hour. Then you talk in the other language for half an hour. This way, both people get the same time to learn.

In this lesson, you visit the Dutch Language Café in The Hague. The Hague is a city in the Netherlands. On Thursday evenings, its language exchange starts at 19:30. When you arrive, you tell the host: I'm here for the language exchange.

But how do you find the right person in a busy room? The café gives you a language sticker. You write your languages on it and wear it. You write your native language and the languages you want to practise. Your native language is the first language you learned as a child. Other people look at your sticker and know your languages at once. They often ask too: Which languages do you speak? You answer: My native language is Japanese.

At 20:00, the speed exchange starts. You talk with one person for 3 minutes. Then you move to the next person. Why so short? This way, you meet many people quickly. Later, everyone sits at language tables, for example an English table.

Part 2: Ordering at the Café

At the Dutch Language Café, the Thursday ticket costs €11. The ticket pays for snacks and games. The snacks are things like popcorn and nuts. You ask the host: Are the snacks included? The host says yes. But drinks are not in the ticket, so you buy them at the bar.

On the menu, you see a funny name: koffie verkeerd. In English, koffie verkeerd is wrong coffee. Why wrong? A normal Dutch coffee has only a little milk. Koffie verkeerd has more milk than coffee, so it is the wrong way around. At the bar, you order: Can I have a koffie verkeerd, please? Then you ask: How much is it? It costs €4.00.

If you are hungry, you ask the waiter: What is an empanada? It is a small pastry, with cheese, meat or vegetables inside. An empanada costs €6.50.

Part 3: Introducing Yourself

In the Netherlands, people shake hands when they meet someone new. At the same time, they say their name: Hi! I'm Sanne. Often they say only their first name. In Japan, many people bow, or bend forward, and say their family name. But for Dutch people, a hello without a name is rude, or not polite. Why? Dutch people like to be clear, so they start with a name. When you hear a new name, you say your name too. Then you say: Nice to meet you.

The first time you meet a Dutch person, do not ask: How are you? Many Dutch people use this question only with people they already know. A stranger is a person you do not know. If a stranger asks this, a Dutch person may think you met before. They may think they forgot you!

In a 3-minute speed exchange, you do not have much time. So say three things: your name, your city and why you study the language. Lucía from Spain says: Hi! I'm Lucía. I'm from Valencia. I study Dutch because I work in The Hague. The last part is the most useful. It gives people a question to ask, for example: Where do you work? Then Lucía asks: What about you?

Part 4: Keeping the Conversation Going

The best tool for a long conversation is the follow-up question. This is a question about what the other person just said. Lucía says: I work at a hotel. A new question jumps to a new idea: Do you like football? A follow-up question stays with her idea: What do you do at the hotel?

Lucía also says: I moved here from Valencia. Each follow-up question uses one part of what she says. You ask about her reason: Really? Why did you move here? You ask about her city: What is Valencia like? You ask about time: How long have you lived here?

In 2017, scientists at Harvard University studied how strangers talk. People who asked more questions were liked more by their partners. Follow-up questions worked best of all. Why? A follow-up question shows that you are really listening.

At 21:30, the exchange ends. When you want to leave, end the conversation in a kind way. Do not just walk away. The other person gave you their time. So say thank you with kind words: It was nice to talk to you.

Conversation: Arriving and Ordering

Host

Good evening! Are you here for the language exchange?

You

Yes, I'm here for the language exchange. I signed up online.

Host

Great. Here is your sticker. Please write your languages on it.

You

OK. My native language is Japanese, and I study English.

Host

Perfect. Anything else?

You

Are the snacks included?

Host

Yes, the snacks are free. Drinks are not included. You buy them at the bar.

Waiter

Hi! What would you like?

You

Can I have a koffie verkeerd, please?

Waiter

Sure. Anything to eat?

You

What is an empanada?

Waiter

It's a small pastry, with cheese, meat or vegetables inside.

You

A cheese one, please. How much is it?

Waiter

The coffee is €4.00, and the empanada is €6.50. So €10.50, please.

You

Here you go. Thank you!

Yes, that's right! You learn fast.

Speak

Speaking

Find a partner. One of you is the visitor. The other person plays the host, the waiter, Sanne, Daan and Lucía. Practise the evening at the Dutch Language Café again. Get your sticker, and order a koffie verkeerd and a cheese empanada for €10.50. Shake hands, and say your name, your city and why you study English. Ask at least three follow-up questions. Then change roles and use your real life.
