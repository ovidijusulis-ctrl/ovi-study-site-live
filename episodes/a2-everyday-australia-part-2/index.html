Part 1: A Lake in the Middle of Canberra

Canberra is the capital of Australia, and it is a planned city. People drew the city on paper first, and then they built it. Why? When Australia became one country, Sydney and Melbourne both wanted to be the capital. They could not agree, so the government built a new city between them.

In 1912, two Americans won a competition to draw the new city. They were a husband and wife, Walter Burley Griffin and Marion Mahony Griffin. For the Griffins, a lake was the heart of the city. The government buildings are on one side of the lake. The city centre is on the other side. They look at each other across the water.

But Canberra had only a small river. So workers built a dam, a big wall that stops a river. In 1963, they closed its gates. The river water stopped at the wall, and slowly it made a lake. By April 1964, the lake was full. It is 11 kilometres long, and its name is Lake Burley Griffin.

Today the lake is the weekend park for the city. The most popular walk and bike ride is the bridge-to-bridge loop. It is a 5-kilometre circle that goes over two bridges. People from rowing clubs train in long, thin boats at the west end. By the water there are free electric barbecues and picnic tables. So families bring food and cook their lunch there.

Part 2: Coffee and the Flat White

Australians love coffee, and this love started with migrants. Migrants are people who move to live in a new country. After 1945, many people came to Australia from Italy and Greece. They brought espresso machines with them. These machines push hot water through coffee very fast. They make a small, strong coffee called an espresso. Greek families put the machines in their milk bars, small shops for drinks and snacks. Italian families opened espresso cafés in Melbourne.

The most famous Australian coffee is the flat white. It is espresso with hot milk and a little foam on top. Foam is milk with a lot of air in it. There is very little foam, so the top looks flat. Australia and New Zealand both say they made the first one, in the 1980s. Today a flat white costs about $6 in a big city café.

Most cafés in Australia are small local shops, not big chains. A chain is a company with the same shop in many places. Australians already had good local cafés, so big chains found it hard. In 2008, Starbucks closed more than 60 of its shops in Australia. People said its drinks were too sweet and too expensive.

Part 3: The Sausage Sizzle and Election Day

A sausage sizzle is a barbecue that sells food to raise money for a group. Sizzle is the sound of food cooking on a hot plate. You get one sausage in a slice of white bread. Many people add fried onions and tomato sauce.

The most famous ones are outside Bunnings, a big hardware shop. A hardware shop sells tools and things for your house and garden. On weekends, a local group, like a school or a sports club, cooks there. Bunnings takes no money from the group, so the group keeps it all. A sausage costs $3.50 and a drink costs $2.

Sausage sizzles also appear on election day, the day people vote. They are at polling places, the places where people vote. Why there? In Australia, voting is compulsory. Every Australian citizen aged 18 or older must vote. People who do not vote can get a fine. A fine is money you pay when you break a rule. Because of this law, more than 90% of people vote in national elections.

Elections are on Saturdays, and people often vote in schools. Almost everyone comes, so school parents cook for the long lines. The money helps the school. Australians call this a democracy sausage. In a democracy, people choose their leaders by voting. In 2016, Australian word experts made it their Word of the Year.

Part 4: The Right to Disconnect

Many people get work emails and messages at night. In Australia, a law now helps with this. It is called the right to disconnect. When you disconnect from work, you stop all work, even on your phone.

The law started on 26 August 2024 for companies with 15 or more workers. From 26 August 2025, it also covers small businesses. So now almost every worker has this right.

After work, you can choose not to read or answer work messages. Your boss can still send them, but you can wait until your next work day. There is one rule: your choice must not be unreasonable. Unreasonable is another way to say not fair. For example, at 9:30 pm your manager emails to move tomorrow's meeting. It is not urgent: it does not need an answer now. So you can read it in the morning.

But sometimes waiting is not fair, and you should answer. For example, someone could get hurt. Or your job pays you extra to be on call. When you are on call, you are ready to work at any time.

Why do Australians care so much? Many people work extra hours for no money. This is called unpaid overtime. In 2025, a study found that workers did 3.6 hours of unpaid overtime every week. That is 173 hours a year, or more than four weeks of free work. Australians love their evenings and weekends. They use them for sport, the beach and family. This law helps them keep those hours free.

Scene

Scene: Saturday at the Lake

It is Saturday morning in Canberra. You meet your Australian friend Jack at Lake Burley Griffin. There is a coffee van by the water. The local rowing club has a sausage sizzle too.

Jack uses some Australian words. Brekkie is breakfast, and arvo is afternoon. Mate is another word for friend. No worries is a way to say it's OK. Your goal: get a coffee and a sausage, and choose what to do next.

Conversation: Saturday at the Lake

Jack

Morning, mate! Have you had brekkie?

You

Not yet. Just a coffee for now.

Jack

Good idea. The coffee van is right there.

Café worker

Morning! What can I get you?

You

Can I get a flat white, please?

Café worker

Sure. Have here or take away?

You

Take away, thanks. How much is that?

Café worker

Six dollars.

You

Here you go.

Café worker

No worries. It'll be ready in two minutes.

You

Jack, people say mate a lot here.

Jack

Yeah, we say it to everyone. Look, the rowing club has a sausage sizzle.

You

What's it for?

Club member

We're raising money for a new boat.

You

How much is a sausage?

Club member

Three fifty. A drink is two dollars.

You

One sausage with onions, please. And a water.

Club member

That's five fifty.

You

Here's six dollars. Keep the change.

Club member

Thanks, mate! The sauce is on the table.

Jack

My boss just sent me an email.

You

On a Saturday? Do you have to answer?

Jack

No, it's not urgent. I'm off work now.

Jack

I'll get back to my boss on Monday.

You

Good! So what do we do this arvo?

Jack

Do you want to go for a walk? The bridge-to-bridge loop is five kilometres, over two bridges.

You

How long does it take?

Jack

About an hour. Then let's have a picnic. The barbecues are free.

You

Sounds great!

Jack

No worries, mate.

Speaking

Find a partner. One person is the visitor, and the other is an Australian friend. Play the Saturday at the lake again, but change the details. Order a different coffee and ask what the sausage sizzle money is for. Choose something different for the afternoon. Your friend's boss sends a message, so talk about the right to disconnect. Then change roles.
