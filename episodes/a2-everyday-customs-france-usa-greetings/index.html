Everyday Customs in France and the USA

Introduction

First, we look at greetings and how the relationship between two people decides the greeting in France and the USA. We also see what to do about shoes when you visit an American home.

Next, we learn why one small French word, "Bonjour," comes before every request. Then we compare tipping, and we finish with table manners and public transport, where both countries ask you to wait for others.

Before you begin

Warm-up

  1. How do you usually greet a friend, and how do you greet a new person at work?

  2. Do you leave extra money for a waiter in your country? Why or why not?

  3. Think of a time when a visitor did something that felt a little strange to you. What was it?

Reading

Part 1: The Relationship Chooses the Greeting

Imagine one French woman on one day. In the morning, she meets a new client at the office, and they shake hands. In the evening, she arrives at her sister's birthday dinner, and she greets everyone with la bise, the cheek kiss from the last lesson. She is the same person, but she uses two different greetings. What changed? Only the relationship.

In the USA, people often say "Hello" and shake hands, especially in professional places like an office. Some people do not feel comfortable with a handshake. For them, there is a polite choice: they can put a hand on their chest and bow a little. You may remember the bows from Japan in the last lesson. Here, a small bow is not the main custom. It is a polite way to say hello without touching. American manners also change from place to place and from one situation to another.

This is also true when you visit an American home. Some families take off their shoes at the door, and some do not. You remember the slippers in Japan, but there is not one rule for every American home. So after you say "Hello," look at the shoes near the door. If you are not sure, ask a simple question: "Should I take off my shoes?" A short question is polite, and it is better than guessing.

So a greeting is not one fixed rule for a whole country. It is more like choosing clothes: you dress one way for work and another way for a family party. When you know the relationship and the situation, you can usually choose the right greeting.

Check: Part 1: The Relationship Chooses the Greeting

1. Why does the same French woman shake hands in the morning but do la bise in the evening?

Sample answer

Because the greeting depends on the relationship: she shakes hands with a new client at work, and she uses la bise with family and friends.

Think one step further

Which greeting would feel most comfortable for you at a first meeting in France or the USA, and why?

Reading

Part 2: "Bonjour" Opens the Door

Picture a visitor in a small Paris shop. She walks in and asks at once, "Excuse me, where is the bread?" Her words are polite. But the shop worker may answer a little coldly. Why?

In France, it is important to say "Bonjour" before any request in a shop or café. In the evening, people say "Bonsoir." Many people add "Monsieur" or "Madame," for example "Bonjour, Madame." This greeting is not extra. It is the first step of the request. Without it, the question can feel like an order, as if you spoke to a machine and not to a person.

The same respect appears in other words. French has two words for "you." People use the polite vous with strangers and at work. They change to the friendly tu only when the other person invites them. In formal situations or with older people, people use "Madame" or "Monsieur" and family names. They wait until they are invited to use first names.

In the USA, "Hello" is also a normal and polite greeting. But in France, the greeting does a bigger job. It tells the other person, "I see you before I ask you for something." So the visitor in the shop did not use bad words. She only skipped the step that comes first.

1. Why might a French shop worker answer coldly when a visitor asks a polite question without saying "Bonjour" first?

Sample answer

Because in France, "Bonjour" is part of a polite request. Without it, the question can feel like an order, even when the words are polite.

Think one step further

Reading

Part 3: Tipping: Respect Paid at the End

Now imagine the end of a meal. In France and in the USA, you pay the bill. But what about the tip, the extra money for the waiter?

In the USA, a tip is expected in service jobs. For good service, people usually add 15–20% of the bill. That means about $1.50 to $2 for every $10 on the bill. Visitors sometimes see this as a gift. But many Americans see it as a normal part of the bill, so leaving nothing can send a strong message.

In France, tipping is optional. People do not have to leave a tip, but waiters are happy to get one for excellent service. So the same action, leaving no tip, can mean very different things. In France it is normal. In the USA it can look like you were unhappy with the service.

This is a good example of how respect moves to a different place. A French customer shows respect with "Bonjour" at the start. An American customer often shows it with money at the end. A visitor who brings only one country's habits can easily get one of these moments wrong.

Check: Part 3: Tipping: Respect Paid at the End

1. Why can leaving no tip mean something different in the USA and in France?

Sample answer

In the USA, a tip of 15–20% is expected for good service, so no tip can look like you were unhappy. In France, tipping is optional, so no tip is normal.

Think one step further

Do you think a tip should be expected, or should it be only for excellent service? Explain your choice.

Reading

Part 4: After You: Waiting for the Group

Think about two everyday places: a dinner table and a bus door. They look very different, but in both places the same question appears: do you go first, or do you wait for others?

At an American table, people usually wait until everyone has their food before they start eating. They put a napkin on their lap and chew with their mouth closed. They also stay at the table until everyone has finished. Starting early or leaving early shows that you are thinking only about yourself.

Public transport follows the same idea. In the USA, cutting in line is rude. People usually wait their turn in a queue and when they get on a bus or train. In France, people also let passengers get off before they get on. They greet the bus driver when they get on, which is the "Bonjour" habit again. They give their seat to people who need it more, and they avoid loud noise.

So the table and the bus share one rule: do not put yourself ahead of the group. The details change, but the idea stays the same. Wait for the others to be served, wait for people to get off, and keep your voice low so others can travel in peace.

What you can do now

Final Reflection

At the start, it seemed easy to think that good manners are the same everywhere. In fact, France and the USA both value respect, but they show it in different moments: a "Bonjour" before a question, a tip at the end of a meal, or a step back at the bus door. A polite visitor does not need new values, only a new sense of where respect goes.

  1. Which French or American custom from today would be the easiest for you, and which would be the hardest to remember?

  2. If a French or American visitor came to your country, what is one custom you would explain to them first, and why?
