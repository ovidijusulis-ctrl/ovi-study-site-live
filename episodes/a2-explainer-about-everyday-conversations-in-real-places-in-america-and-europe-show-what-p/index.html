Before you begin

Warm-up

  1. Where do you talk to strangers most often: on a train, in a line, or in a park?

  2. What do you say first to a new coworker?

  3. Do you talk more in the morning or in the evening?

Reading

Part 1: New York City subway commute

Small talk is short, friendly talk about light things, like the weather. In New York City, a lot of it happens on the move.

The New York City Subway carries millions of riders every day, and the cars are often crowded. People stand close, but most stay quiet with strangers.

When they do speak, the pace is fast and the topic is practical. A rider asks which train stops at a station, or mentions another delay.

Weather comes up, but in a useful way: it is raining, so the walk from the station will be wet. Here is the lens for the whole lesson: place, pace, and topic.

On the subway, the place is tight, the pace is quick, and the topic is getting somewhere. A morning train in Tokyo feels similar in that way.

Check: Part 1: New York City subway commute

1. Why is subway talk in New York City short and practical?

Sample answer

The car is crowded and people are traveling to work or school, so they mostly stay quiet, and when they talk it is about delays, which train to take, or the weather for the walk outside.

Think one step further

Do you talk to anyone on your way to work or school?

What do you say when a train is late where you live?

Do you find the weather useful on your commute, or just friendly?

Reading

Part 2: London cafe queue

In London, people wait in queues, and a queue is a line. The queue is orderly, and standing in it is a shared moment.

This is where small talk fits well. The place is calm, the pace is medium, and the topic is often the weather.

Someone mentions that it is cold today, and the next person agrees and adds that it may rain later. These are short turns, a sentence or two each, and then the talk stops when the coffee arrives.

Politeness matters in United Kingdom culture, so people use please, thank you, and sorry often, even in a two-line chat. Coffee culture is social in many countries, but the London queue version is light and quick. It feels warmer than the subway car because nobody is rushing to a door.

1. How is the London cafe queue different from the New York City subway for small talk?

Sample answer

The queue is calm and orderly, so people have a shared waiting moment and make short polite comments, often about the weather, while subway riders are moving fast and mostly stay quiet.

Think one step further

Do you say sorry or thank you a lot when you wait in line?

What do you usually say about today's weather?

Reading

Part 3: Madrid plaza life in the evening

Madrid is the capital of Spain, and much of its social life happens outdoors in the evening. Plaza Mayor is a big historic square in the center of Madrid, and it works as a public meeting place.

Smaller neighborhood plazas do the same job for the people who live nearby. Now apply the lens.

The place is open and shared, the pace is slow, and the topic is people. Friends and families sit outside, and the talk lasts an hour or more.

Food is a natural topic: what to eat tonight, and which bar has good snacks. Weekend plans come up, and so do family news and children.

This is the opposite end from the subway. Nobody is going anywhere, so the talk can grow long and relaxed.

People come to the plaza to sit and meet in the evening, not to travel, so the slow pace lets them talk for a long time about food, family, and weekend plans.

Reading

Part 4: Berlin office kitchen

Berlin is the capital of Germany, and it is a working city with a normal workday rhythm. The office kitchen is where short workday talk happens.

People come in for coffee, and that gives them a reason to stand together for a minute. Conversation norms in Germany are direct and practical, so the talk is clear and to the point.

Someone asks about the meeting at ten, or mentions that the project is behind. Weekend plans appear on Friday: a trip, a birthday, or just rest.

Weather is fine as a topic, but it usually leads to a plan, like taking the bike if it stays dry. Under the lens, the place is a work space, the pace is quick, and the topic is the day. The talk ends when the cup is full.

Check: Part 4: Berlin office kitchen

1. What kind of talk fits a Berlin office kitchen?

Sample answer

Short, direct, practical talk about the workday, like a meeting or a project, plus simple weekend plans, all while people get coffee for a minute.

Think one step further

Do you chat during short breaks at work or at school?

Where do you take short breaks at work or school?

What do you usually say about your weekend on a Monday?

Reading

Part 5: Paris bakery line

Paris is the capital of France, and its mornings often start at a bakery. A bakery is a shop that sells bread and pastries, and in the morning a line forms outside.

The place is busy, the pace is quick, and the topic is food first. People name the bread they want, and they comment on the croissants being fresh or the good ones being gone.

Talk with a neighbor in line is a short turn, often about morning plans: work, school, or a visit. The bakery line is like the London queue in shape, but the topic moves from weather to bread.

In Japan, a quick word at a station bakery or a convenience store counter plays a similar role. It is talk that fits between two other tasks.

Reading

Part 6: Same needs, different talk by place

This lesson looks at everyday conversations in real places in America with clear city examples. Cities in Europe give more examples of the same daily talk. These chats happen in named settings that learners can picture. This shows how talk differs by place while needs stay familiar.

Put the five places side by side with the lens. The human needs are the same everywhere: weather, food, work or school, and weekend plans. The place changes what comes first and how long it lasts. On the New York City subway, weather is about a wet walk, and the talk lasts seconds. In a London cafe queue, weather is the polite opening, and the talk lasts a minute. In a Madrid plaza, food and family fill an evening. In a Berlin office kitchen, work and coffee fill a short break. In a Paris bakery line, bread fills the moment. Fast, tight, task places make short practical talk. Slow, open, social places make long personal talk. If you know the place and the pace, you can guess the topic.
