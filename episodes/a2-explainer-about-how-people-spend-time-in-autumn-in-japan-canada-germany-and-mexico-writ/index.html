Warm-up

  2. Do you like spending autumn time at home or outside?

  3. What autumn events happen where you live?

Reading

Part 1: Moon Viewing in Japan

Autumn activities are easier to understand with three questions: When do they happen? Where do people spend time? What does the activity mean? We can use these questions in all four countries.

To see how people spend time in autumn in Japan, start with Tsukimi, the Japanese autumn moon-viewing custom. Jugoya is the fifteenth night of the eighth lunar month. In 2026, Tsukimi is on 25 September. People display dango, round rice dumplings, and susuki, or silver grass. The round dango stand for the full moon. The grass stands for rice and good crops.

Part 2: Tsukimi in Daily Life

Now add place and daily routine to our three questions. Many people enjoy Tsukimi at home under the moonlight. It is a custom, not a national public holiday. There are no holiday days connected with the moon festival, so work continues as normal. Offices and shops stay open.

Businesses may still mark the season with special foods and marketing. This shows how a meaningful custom can be part of an ordinary working day. People do not need a whole day away from work to notice the season and enjoy the moon.

Part 3: Autumn Colour in Canada

In Canada, people spend time in autumn looking at fall colour and marking Thanksgiving. Fall colour means leaves changing colour in autumn. Algonquin Park, a large provincial park in Ontario, is one place where people can see this change.

The colour of Sugar Maple and Red Maple leaves is usually strongest in the last week of September or the first week of October. Canadian Thanksgiving is on the second Monday in October, and some park services stay open until that weekend. In our three-question lens, the place is a park, the time is early autumn, and the changing leaves are the main natural event.

Part 4: A Large Festival in Germany

In Germany, people spend autumn days at Oktoberfest, Munich’s big autumn festival. Munich is a city in Bavaria. The 191st Oktoberfest will take place at Theresienwiese, the festival ground in Munich, from 19 September to 4 October 2026.

This is a very large public event. About 6.5 million guests visited in 2025. Its history began with a royal marriage celebration on fields outside Munich on 12 October 1810. Horse races happened again the next year, and the event grew into Oktoberfest. Here, autumn time is spent in a busy public place rather than at home.

Part 5: Welcoming the Dead in Mexico

In Mexico, people spend time in autumn preparing for Día de Muertos, the Mexican Day of the Dead festivity. It happens each year from the end of October to the beginning of November. It marks the short return of family members who have died.

Families make an ofrenda, a home offering or shrine for the dead. They place favourite dishes, flowers, candles, and handmade objects around home shrines and tombs. Flower petals and candles can also mark paths. The period comes at the end of the yearly maize, or corn, growing cycle. UNESCO put this living tradition on its Intangible Cultural Heritage list in 2008.

Part 6: Comparing Autumn Time

Our three questions show important differences. In Japan, many people view the moon at home while normal work continues. In Canada, people look at changing leaves in places such as Algonquin Park and mark Thanksgiving. In Germany, people attend a large public festival in Munich. In Mexico, families prepare home shrines and tombs for relatives who have died.

The places are different: a home, a park, a festival ground, or a tomb. The times are also different, from late September to early November. However, each activity gives people a way to notice the autumn season and spend time with a custom, place, or memory.
