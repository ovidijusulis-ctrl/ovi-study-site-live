Before you begin

Warm-up

  1. What do you always take on a school trip?

  2. What is one rule at your school or work?

  3. Do you like choosing your own activities?

Reading

Part 1: Have To and Has To

Your class is getting ready for a trip.

An action is necessary when you must do it. Use have to for necessary actions. Use have to with I, you, we and they. Use has to with he, she, it or one person's name. Add a base verb: the simple form, like bring.

Start a question with Do or Does. Then put the person before have to + base verb. Use Do with I, you, we and they. Use Does with he, she, it or one name. In a short answer, use do or does without repeating the action.

I have to bring lunch.

Do you have to bring lunch? Yes, I do. / No, I do not.

Mia has to carry the class bag.

Does she have to carry the class bag? Yes, she does. / No, she does not.

Mia does not have to bring a camera.

When someone asks you about yourself, answer with I. For Mia, use she; for a boy's name, use he. For a full negative, use do not have to or does not have to. The action is not necessary. Do not becomes don't; does not becomes doesn't.

After does or doesn't, have stays have, never has. Do not add am, is or are before have to.

    Part 2: Must and Must Not

    Before the class leaves, the teacher gives the rules.

    Must is also for necessary actions. Use it when you tell someone what they must do. You also see it in written rules. Use person + must + base verb. Must stays the same with I, you, he, she, it, we and they.

    For questions, put must before the person. For an answer with yes, use the person + must. For negatives, use must not or mustn't: do not do the action.

    We must stay together.

    Must we stay together? Yes, you must.

    She must listen to the teacher.

    She must not open the bus door.

    He mustn't open the bus door.

    The class asks the teacher, so we becomes you in the answer. When she changes to he, must and must not stay the same.

    Do not add do or does to a must question. Use Must we wait here?, never Do we must wait here? Questions with have to are more common in everyday conversation.

      Reading

      Part 3: Must Not or Do Not Have To

      The class visits a museum. Its rules forbid touching the old things. To forbid something is to say it is not allowed. The museum also offers a drawing activity. Joining is optional: you can choose.

      Use must not for a forbidden action. Use do not have to when an action is not necessary. Use doesn't have to with he, she or it.

      You must not touch the old things.

      She mustn't touch the old things.

      You don't have to join the drawing activity.

      She doesn't have to join the drawing activity.

      Must I join the drawing activity? No, you do not have to.

      The last answer gives you a choice: you can join. An answer with must not says joining is forbidden. It cannot mean that joining is optional. In everyday conversation, Do I have to join? is more common than Must I join?

      Do not use don't have to for the museum's rule about touching. It says touching is not necessary. It does not say touching is forbidden.

        Reading

        Part 4: The Base Verb after a Modal

        The teacher checks the class before the journey home. Now look at the action words.

        Must is a modal: a helping verb with its own form rules. After must, use the base verb. Do not add to or an ending for he or she.

        She must carry the bag.

        He must write his name.

        We must leave together.

        You must listen carefully.

        She must close the door.

        The spelling stays carry, write, leave, listen and close after must. Keep the y in carry. Keep the final e in write, leave and close. Listen has a t, although you do not hear it.

        Compare She has to carry the bag and She doesn't have to carry the bag. Have changes to has in the first sentence. After doesn't, use have. In both sentences, carry stays in its base form. A common mistake is adding s to carry after must.
