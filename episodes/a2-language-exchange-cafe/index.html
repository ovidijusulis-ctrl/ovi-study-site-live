Language Exchange Café: Meeting New People

Quality checked lesson

Published 14 Nov 2026

At a café language exchange, you trade languages with new people: you read their stickers, shake hands, say your first name, keep the talk going with follow-up questions and your own stories, and end in a kind way.

Before you begin

Warm-up

  1. Do you like meeting new people? Why or why not?

  2. What do you usually say first when you meet someone new?

  3. Which language would you like to practise with a native speaker?

Reading

Part 1: How a Language Exchange Works

A language exchange is a trade: you give something, and you get something back. You help someone with your language, and they help you with theirs. For example, you speak Japanese and want to practise English. So you look for an English speaker who wants to practise Japanese.

Everyone is a teacher and a student at the same time. When you help with your language, you are the teacher. When the other person helps you, you are the student.

In this lesson, you visit the Dutch Language Café in The Hague. The Hague is a city in the Netherlands. The café has a language exchange every Thursday evening.

Every visitor gets a language sticker and wears it on their shirt. Write your languages on two lines, so people can see the difference. Your native language is the first language you learned as a child. It goes on the Speak line. For example:

  • Speak: Japanese
  • Practise: English

A sticker shows you who can help you. The room is full of strangers, people you do not know. But with one look at a sticker, you know their languages. It also gives you your first question. Do you see Spanish on the Practise line? Ask: I see you study Spanish. Why?

The evening has a clear plan:

  • 19:30: arrive and get your sticker.
  • 20:00: speed exchange, 3 minutes with each person.
  • 20:30: language tables, with one language at each table.
  • 21:10: change tables if you want.

In the speed exchange, you take turns choosing the language. First one of you chooses, then the other, so both people practise. After 3 minutes, you move to the next person. Why so short? In a short time, you meet many new people.

Part 2: Ordering at the Café

Your €11 ticket includes the snacks and the games. Included means they are in the price. The snacks are popcorn, crisps and nuts. You eat them at your table.

Drinks are not included, so you buy them at the bar. You order from the bartender, the person who works behind the bar. You buy food there too.

On the menu, you see a funny name: koffie verkeerd. In English, it means wrong coffee. Why wrong? A normal Dutch coffee has only a little milk. Koffie verkeerd has a lot of warm milk, often more than the coffee. So it is the wrong way around.

If you are hungry, try an empanada. It is small and baked, with food inside. At this café, you choose cheese, meat or spinach.

When you do not understand, do not just say yes. A bar is loud, and a menu has new words. Ask: Sorry, can you say that again? Or ask about one word: What does spinach mean? At a language café, everyone is a student, so questions are normal.

Part 3: Introducing Yourself

In the Netherlands, people shake hands with someone new and say their name. Often it is only the first name: Hi! I'm Sanne. In Japan, many people bow, or bend forward, and say their family name.

A hello without your name is rude, or not polite. Why? Your name tells people who you are. It also tells them what to call you all evening. So when you hear a new name, say your name too.

At a first meeting, do not ask: How are you? For many Dutch people, it is a real question, not just a hello. They ask it when they really want to know the answer. So they usually ask people they already know.

Save How are you? for next Thursday, when you meet again. At a first meeting, just say hello and your name.

A speed exchange is short. So say three things: your name, your city and why you study the language. Daan says: Hi! I'm Daan. I live in The Hague. I study Japanese because I'm going to Tokyo.

The reason is the most useful part. It gives the other person a question. For example: What do you want to see in Tokyo? When you finish, ask back: What about you?

Part 4: Keeping the Conversation Going

The best tool for a long conversation is the follow-up question. A follow-up question is a question about what the other person just said. It stays with their idea. A new question jumps to a new idea, for example: Do you like football?

Each answer gives you the next follow-up question:

  • Lucía: I moved here from Valencia.
  • You: Really? Why did you move here?
  • Lucía: For work. I work at a hotel.
  • You: What do you do at the hotel?

In 2017, scientists at Harvard University studied conversations between strangers. People who asked more questions were liked more by their partners. Follow-up questions worked best. Why? A follow-up question shows that you are really listening.

But a good conversation goes both ways. If you never talk about yourself, people may think you are hiding something. So react to what you hear: Really? Me too! or That sounds fun! Then tell something about yourself, and ask back.

Part 5: Ending Well

At 21:30, the exchange ends. Do not just walk away. The other person gave you their time. So thank them with kind words: It was nice to talk to you.

Do you want to keep in touch? Ask for WhatsApp. Keep in touch means you talk or write again later. In the Netherlands, WhatsApp is by far the most used app for messages. So almost everyone you meet can say yes.

The exchange is every Thursday, so say goodbye for one week: See you next Thursday! Next time, you can ask: How are you?

In the speed exchange, you take turns choosing the language.

First one of you chooses, then the other, so both people practise.

After 3 minutes, you move to the next person.

Why so short?

In a short time, you meet many new people.

Reading

Part 2: Ordering at the Café

Your €11 ticket includes the snacks and the games.

Included means they are in the price.

The snacks are popcorn, crisps and nuts.

You eat them at your table.

Drinks are not included, so you buy them at the bar.

You order from the bartender, the person who works behind the bar.

You buy food there too.

On the menu, you see a funny name: koffie verkeerd.

In English, it means wrong coffee.

Why wrong?

A normal Dutch coffee has only a little milk.

Koffie verkeerd has a lot of warm milk, often more than the coffee.

So it is the wrong way around.

If you are hungry, try an empanada.

It is small and baked, with food inside.

At this café, you choose cheese, meat or spinach.

When you do not understand, do not just say yes.

A bar is loud, and a menu has new words.

Ask: Sorry, can you say that again?

Or ask about one word: What does spinach mean?

At a language café, everyone is a student, so questions are normal.

Reading

Part 3: Introducing Yourself

In the Netherlands, people shake hands with someone new and say their name.

Often it is only the first name: Hi!

I'm Sanne.

In Japan, many people bow, or bend forward, and say their family name.

A hello without your name is rude, or not polite.

Why?

Your name tells people who you are.

It also tells them what to call you all evening.

So when you hear a new name, say your name too.

At a first meeting, do not ask: How are you?

For many Dutch people, it is a real question, not just a hello.

They ask it when they really want to know the answer.

So they usually ask people they already know.

Save How are you?

for next Thursday, when you meet again.

At a first meeting, just say hello and your name.

A speed exchange is short.

So say three things: your name, your city and why you study the language.

Daan says: Hi!

I'm Daan.

I live in The Hague.

I study Japanese because I'm going to Tokyo.

The reason is the most useful part.

It gives the other person a question.

For example: What do you want to see in Tokyo?

When you finish, ask back: What about you?

Reading

Part 4: Keeping the Conversation Going

The best tool for a long conversation is the follow-up question.

A follow-up question is a question about what the other person just said.

It stays with their idea.

A new question jumps to a new idea, for example: Do you like football?

Each answer gives you the next follow-up question: Lucía: I moved here from Valencia.

You: Really?

Why did you move here?

Lucía: For work.

I work at a hotel.

You: What do you do at the hotel?

In 2017, scientists at Harvard University studied conversations between strangers.

People who asked more questions were liked more by their partners.

Follow-up questions worked best.

Why?

A follow-up question shows that you are really listening.

But a good conversation goes both ways.

If you never talk about yourself, people may think you are hiding something.

So react to what you hear: Really?

Me too!

or That sounds fun!

Then tell something about yourself, and ask back.

Reading

Part 5: Ending Well

At 21:30, the exchange ends.

Do not just walk away.

The other person gave you their time.

So thank them with kind words: It was nice to talk to you.

Do you want to keep in touch?

Ask for WhatsApp.

Keep in touch means you talk or write again later.

In the Netherlands, WhatsApp is by far the most used app for messages.

So almost everyone you meet can say yes.

The exchange is every Thursday, so say goodbye for one week: See you next Thursday!

Next time, you can ask: How are you?
