Reading

Part 1: Vilnius Old Town and Cepelinai

Your trip starts in Vilnius, the capital of Lithuania. You walk through the Old Town. The streets are small, and there are old churches everywhere. This Old Town is very old, and it is also very big. Many of its buildings and streets are hundreds of years old. Because of this, UNESCO put it on its World Heritage list. UNESCO is a world group that takes care of very important places.

At lunch, you go into Etno Dvaras, a restaurant with food from Lithuania. You order cepelinai, the national dish of Lithuania. Cepelinai are big potato dumplings. A dumpling is a soft ball of food with more food inside. Inside cepelinai, there is usually meat or curd cheese, a soft white cheese. On top, there is a sauce of sour cream with small pieces of bacon.

Why the funny name? A zeppelin is a long, round flying ship. It carries people, like a plane. Cepelinai have the same long, round shape. So people named them after the zeppelin. At Etno Dvaras, one plate of cepelinai costs 9.55 euros.

Part 2: Trakai Island Castle and Kibinai

The next day, you take a train from Vilnius to Trakai. Trakai Island Castle stands on a small island in a lake. You walk to it over a bridge. The castle is red and more than 600 years old. Inside, there is a museum about the history of Lithuania.

After the castle, you go to Karaimų Street to try kibinai. Kibinai are small pastries. Inside, there is meat and onion. The traditional meat is mutton, the meat of an adult sheep. Kibinai are the food of the Karaim people. In English, Karaimų Street is Street of the Karaim.

Who are the Karaim? More than 600 years ago, Vytautas was the ruler of Lithuania. He brought Karaim families to Trakai from Crimea, a place far in the south. Their job was to keep his castle safe. Vytautas gave them land here, and they lived on this street. That is why the street has their name. They brought their own food with them, and kibinai were part of it. A small group of Karaim people still lives in Trakai today.

Part 3: The Hill of Crosses and Šaltibarščiai

On day three, you go north to the city of Šiauliai. The Hill of Crosses is a short drive from the city. From far away, it looks like a small hill. Up close, you see crosses everywhere, big and small, wooden and metal. Today, there are more than 100,000 crosses on the hill.

Why are they here? In the 1800s, many people in Lithuania died in fights against Russia. They wanted their country to be free. Their families never found the bodies. So the families had no place to visit and remember them. Instead, they put crosses on this hill.

Later, Lithuania was part of the Soviet Union. The Soviet leaders did not want people to pray. So they did not want the crosses. Again and again, they destroyed the crosses on the hill. The first time, in 1961, they destroyed all the crosses in one day. Crosses on the hill were against the law, and people were punished for them. But people came back at night and put up new crosses. In the dark, it was harder to see them, but it was still dangerous. They came anyway. So the hill became a sign that the people did not give up.

After your visit, you eat lunch in Šiauliai. It is a hot July day, so you order šaltibarščiai. This is a famous cold pink soup. It is made with beetroot and kefir. Beetroot is a dark red vegetable, and it makes the soup pink. Kefir is a thick milk drink, a little like yogurt. People eat this soup on hot summer days, with boiled potatoes on the side.

Part 4: The Curonian Spit and Smoked Fish

Your last stop is the Curonian Spit. A spit is a long, thin piece of land in the sea. On one side is the Baltic Sea. On the other is the Curonian Lagoon. A lagoon is a big, quiet area of water next to the sea. There is land in between. To get there, you take a short ferry from the city of Klaipėda. It goes to Smiltynė, at the north end of the spit. Then you take a bus to Nida. The ride is about one hour. In the village of Nida, you climb the Parnidis Dune. A dune is a hill of sand that the wind makes. It is 52 metres high. From the top, you see sand, forest and water on both sides.

In the 1800s, fishing was the most important work in Nida. People caught fish in the lagoon. But there were no fridges then, and fish goes bad fast. So they smoked the fish over a fire. Smoke dries the fish, so it stays OK to eat for longer. They often used the wood of the alder tree. Its smoke is soft and sweet, and it makes the fish golden. Today, small shops all over the spit sell smoked fish.

In Nida, you go to a café called Tik pas Joną. In English, the name is Only at Jonas's Place. This café smokes its own fish. You choose bream, a fish from the lagoon, or eel. An eel is a long fish that looks like a snake. The fish is still warm, and you eat it with bread and your hands.

Hi! Excuse me. Can I ask you something?

Yes, of course.

You

How many crosses are there?

Rūta

Nobody can count them all. There are more than 100,000.

To remember someone, or to pray.

You

Is it OK to put up my own cross?

Rūta

Yes. A wooden cross up to 2 metres tall is fine.

You

Do I need to ask anyone first?

Rūta

Not for a small cross. For a taller one, you ask first.

You

Thank you so much!

Speak

Speaking

Work with a partner. Student A is back from a trip to Lithuania. Student B is a friend and asks questions. Student A tells the story of the trip in order, from Vilnius to Nida. For each place, Student A talks about one dish and one fact. Student B asks two questions about each place, like How much was the ticket? Then change roles. After that, act out one scene together, like buying the castle ticket.
