Before you begin

  1. Which everyday activity would you like to try in Australia? What would you like to know about it?

  2. Think of a place near your home where people spend time together. Why do they meet there?

  3. When do you hear informal English, and what helps you understand it?

Reading

Part 1: A Lake Made for a City

Canberra has a lake in the centre of the city. Lake Burley Griffin looks like part of the natural landscape, but people made it. The lake was a central part of Walter Burley Griffin's plan for Canberra.

Today, parks around the water have picnic areas, walking tracks and cycling paths. People can also row, sail, canoe and kayak on the lake. There are clubs for these activities. Tourism Australia gives weekend bike rides around the lake as one example of life in Canberra.

This helps explain an outdoor routine. Enjoying the outdoors needs more than good weather: people need somewhere to go. Here, the city's design gives them places to walk, ride or meet friends. A weekend ride is a personal choice, but the path is something the city provides.

Check: Part 1: A Lake Made for a City

1. How does Canberra's design support outdoor routines?

Sample answer

The planned lake has parks, paths and places for water activities. These give people somewhere to walk, cycle or meet friends.

What makes an outdoor place pleasant to use: its location, paths, trees or something else? Why?

Reading

Part 2: A History in a Cup of Coffee

A familiar drink can tell us something about the people who helped shape a city. After the Second World War, Greek and Italian migrants settled in Melbourne and Sydney. Historian Garritt Van Dyk connects Australia's coffee culture with these communities. Italian communities in regional Queensland also helped shape coffee habits.

The useful point is how a custom travels. People move to a new country and bring familiar habits with them. Over time, those habits can become part of other people's daily lives too. Something that once arrived from elsewhere starts to feel local.

Today, Australian cafés and restaurants serve food from many countries. When you order a coffee, you may simply want a drink or a break with a friend. Behind that ordinary moment is a longer story of people moving and sharing ways of life.

Reading

Part 3: A Sausage with Another Purpose

At a sausage sizzle, people sell cooked sausages in bread. Outside some Australian shops, these simple food sales help local groups raise money.

For example, Canberra Transport Museum advertised a fundraising sausage sizzle at Bunnings Warehouse Belconnen. Its notice invited people to buy food to help pay the museum's ongoing costs: the everyday bills involved in keeping it running.

Bunnings gives another example from Gympie. Sausage-sizzle fundraisers for a Riding for the Disabled Centre helped pay for fences and gardens. Here, selling food supported a practical local project.

The purpose of the purchase matters. You get something to eat, and the sale also supports a group. Compare that with friends sharing a barbecue in a park. The food may be similar, while the reason for gathering is different. Looking at who organises an event and where the money goes tells you more than the food alone.

Reading

Part 4: When Work Reaches Home

A phone can bring work into an evening at home. Australia has a rule called the right to disconnect. Employees can refuse to check, read or answer work contact outside their working hours, unless refusing is unreasonable.

There is an important distinction here. The rule does not ban sending a late message. It concerns whether the worker can reasonably leave it unanswered. The reason for the contact matters, along with how much it disturbs the worker. The person's job, personal circumstances and extra pay for being available also matter. Someone working overtime or on call may still need to respond. Being on call means being available when needed.

The rule shows that protecting free time involves practical choices. A message can wait in some situations and need attention in others. Employers and employees are encouraged to discuss what they expect, so people understand their responsibilities before the phone rings.

Check: Part 4: When Work Reaches Home

1. Does the right to disconnect ban managers from sending late messages?

Sample answer

No. It allows employees to refuse outside-hours contact unless refusing is unreasonable. Responsibilities, the reason for contact, extra pay and personal circumstances can affect this.

Think one step further

Which kinds of work message could usually wait until the next working day? Why?

What would you want to agree with an employer about evening contact?

How do you protect your free time when your phone is nearby?

Conversation: Planning a Barbecue

Friend

Hi! How are you going?

You

Good, thanks. And you?

Friend

Good. We're having a barbie by the lake this arvo. Would you like to come?

You

I'd love to. What can I bring?

Friend

Could you bring a salad? I'll bring the food for the barbecue.

You

No worries. Shall we get brekkie first?

Friend

Yes, I'm hungry. Do you want to go for a walk after breakfast?

You

A short walk sounds good. I've only brought my thongs.

Friend

That's fine. We can choose a place for the picnic.

You

All right. I'll bring the salad when I come back this afternoon.

Friend

Thanks, mate. Let's go and get breakfast.

You

Good idea.

Speak

Speaking

With a partner, make plans for a barbecue. Offer to bring something different and suggest an activity before the meal. Swap roles and try again.

What you can do now

Final Reflection

Public places make activities possible. People bring habits when they move, local groups find ways to support their work, and rules shape expectations about free time. These examples help us understand everyday life through the reasons behind it.

  1. Which example taught you something new about Australia? Explain it in your own words.

  2. Choose a routine from your own life. What place, history or practical need helped shape it?
