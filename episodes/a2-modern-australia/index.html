Australia: Easy Words, Serious Values

Before you begin

Warm-up

  1. What three things come into your head when you think of Australia?

  2. Where did your ideas about Australia come from: films, friends, travel or something else?

  3. How do you talk to a friend? How do you talk to a manager? What is different?

Reading

Part 1: Friendly Words With a Big Meaning

Imagine two short messages from a work friend. The first says, "Good afternoon. I am happy to help you with this task." The second says, "See you this arvo. No worries, mate." Both messages say similar things, but the second one feels warmer. It sounds like a friend, not like a formal letter.

This friendly style is very common in Australia. People use friendly expressions in daily talk. They also often make words shorter. For example, "afternoon" becomes "arvo." A short, friendly form of a word like this is called a diminutive. "No worries" is another friendly phrase. It means "That's fine" or "No problem."

These words are not only fun. They tell us something about values. Australian society cares about informality, which means a relaxed and not-too-formal style. It also cares about equality. Here, equality means that no one should act more important than other people. Friendly words help people feel like equals. They show the idea of "mateship." This means friendship, loyalty and looking after each other.

So when an Australian says "no worries, mate," the words are small, but the message is big. The speaker is saying, "We are on the same level."

Check your understanding

Check: Part 1: Friendly Words With a Big Meaning

1. Why are friendly words like "arvo" and "no worries" more than just fun slang?

Sample answer

They show the informal style that Australian society values. They also connect to mateship and equality. Friendly words help people feel like equals.

Think one step further

Does your language have friendly short words like "arvo"? Who do you use them with?

Do friendly words always make people feel equal? Can you think of a time when they did not?

Would you prefer a manager who says "No worries, mate" or "Certainly, I will help"? Why?

Reading

Part 2: Many Roots, One Table

Think about a family dinner where every person brings one dish from home. At the end, the table has food from many places. Nobody planned it as one menu, but it becomes one shared meal.

Australian culture works a little like that table. But that is not the full story. People have moved to Australia from many different countries. This migration has changed Australian food. It has changed communities too, and the wider culture as well.

So Australia today is a diverse society. Many cultures live side by side and mix with each other. This connects back to Part 1. If a society values equality, it is easier for new people and new foods to find a place at the table. The friendly style and the diverse society belong to the same picture.

There is an important point here. Australian culture is not one fixed thing from the past. It has old roots, but it keeps growing new branches.

Check: Part 2: Many Roots, One Table

1. How has migration changed Australian culture?

Sample answer

Australia has Anglo-Celtic roots, but people from many countries have moved there. This migration has changed its food, communities and culture, so Australia today is a diverse society.

Think one step further

Which foods in your country first came from another culture? Do people still think of them as "foreign"?

How can a new community change a city? Think of a real example you know.

Is a mixed culture stronger or weaker than a culture with one main root? Give a reason.

Reading

Part 3: A Culture That Keeps Changing

Now think about your own phone. In one minute, you can watch a video from another country. You can also see an advert for a product made far away. Your ideas and tastes start to change, often without you noticing.

The same thing happens in Australia. Modern Australian culture is influenced by global communication and advertising. Films, music, online trends and brands from around the world all reach Australian homes. Then people mix these new ideas with local habits and local ways of talking.

This gives us two kinds of change. In Part 2, people moved to Australia and brought their cultures with them. In Part 3, ideas travel to Australia even when people do not move. Both kinds of change make the culture richer.

So if you want to understand modern Australia, a list of old habits is not enough. You need to see a culture that is always meeting the world.

Check: Part 3: A Culture That Keeps Changing

1. What are the two kinds of change described in Parts 2 and 3?

Sample answer

In Part 2, migration brings new cultures when people move to Australia. In Part 3, global communication and advertising bring new ideas, even without people moving.

Think one step further

Which global trends have changed daily life in your country in the last few years?

Can a culture stay "itself" when so many ideas come from outside? What do you think?

Are your ideas about Australia from real life or from films and adverts? How could you check them?

Reading

Part 4: Work: Relaxed Style, Real Values

Imagine your first day in a new office. In one office, everyone calls the boss "Mr" or "Ms" and waits for the boss to speak first. In another office, people use first names and talk to the boss like a team member. Which office feels more equal?

Australian workplaces are usually closer to the second office. They are informal and egalitarian. "Egalitarian" means people are treated as equals, without a strong ladder of who is more important. This is not an accident. Workplaces follow the same values as the wider society. They are informal and they care about equality, just like the friendly language in Part 1.

Here is the key idea. Informal does not mean unimportant. A relaxed style is about how people talk to each other. It does not show how much they care about the work. When people feel equal, they can feel more comfortable to share ideas, ask questions and help each other. A "no worries" can still come before a lot of hard work.

Useful Language

  • arvo — a short, friendly word for "afternoon." Example: "Let's meet this arvo."
  • no worries — a friendly way to say "That's fine" or "No problem." Example: "Thanks for your help!" "No worries."
  • mate — a friend or a friendly way to speak to someone. Example: "Thanks, mate."
  • mateship — friendship, loyalty and looking after each other.
  • informal — relaxed and not too formal, for example using first names.
  • equality — the idea that everyone should be treated as equal.
  • egalitarian — describes a group or place where people are treated as equals.
  • diverse — with many different kinds of people or cultures.
  • migration — people moving from one country to live in another.

They follow the same values as the wider Australian society, which cares about informality and equality.

What you can do now

Final Reflection

At the start, it was easy to think that "casual" means "not serious." Now the picture is different. Australia's friendly words, its mixed and changing culture and its informal workplaces all connect to one serious value: equality. The relaxed style is the surface. Equality and mateship are underneath.

  1. Think of one relaxed habit in your own culture. What serious value could be underneath it?

  2. After this lesson, what would you do differently on your first day in an Australian workplace?
