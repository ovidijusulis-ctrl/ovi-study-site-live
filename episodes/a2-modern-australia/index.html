Before you begin

Warm-up

  1. When you think of Australia, what pictures come into your head first?

  2. At your work or school, do people use first names, titles or family names?

  3. What do you usually do on a free weekend afternoon?

Reading

Part 1: Friendly talk, serious work

Imagine your first week at a new office in Australia. Your manager says, "Call me Chris." In the kitchen, someone you work with asks about your weekend.

Many Australian workplaces are described as relaxed. People often use first names, friendly expressions and short forms. This style can help people feel that they are part of the same team.

But a friendly style does not mean the work is unimportant. In our imagined office, a colleague later offers to help you finish a report on time. Friendly words make it easier to ask questions. The team still expects good work, clear plans and respect for other people's time.

This is a broad picture, not a rule. A hospital, a bank and a building site can feel very different. Some people use a more formal style than others. A good first step is to listen and follow the people around you.

Reading

Part 2: A changing table

Food in Australia today is a mix of many stories. Australia's first and oldest stories belong to Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples. Later, people came from many countries, and migration shaped food and celebrations. Today, restaurants and cafes serve food from many countries. For many people, food is a way to celebrate this mixed identity.

Coffee is a short, clear example. After the Second World War, Greek and Italian migrants settled in Melbourne and Sydney. Their coffee habits became part of city life. Italian communities in regional Queensland also shaped how people drank coffee. So a daily cup of coffee can carry a migration story. The exact origin of the flat white is still unclear.

Home food is often simple and shared. Friends and family gather for a barbecue in a backyard, in a park or at the beach. People may cook sausages, steak, chicken or prawns, and there are often plant-based options too. The meal is about time together, not only the food.

Reading

Part 3: Time outside

Most of Australia's big cities are along the coast. Because of this, many people can go to the beach for a swim or to a park for lunch. On weekends, common social activities include brunch at a cafe, watching or playing sport, and walking in national parks near the city.

Real examples show that outdoor life looks different in different places. In Sydney, some people enjoy swimming in the ocean at sunrise. In Canberra, people take weekend bike rides around the lake. In Perth, people enjoy picnics in Kings Park. So outdoor time is not only about surfing. It can mean a bike, a blanket on the grass or a quiet walk.

There are also limits. These are common activities, not habits of every person. Holiday pictures show sunny, happy moments, but daily life also has work, travel time, family life and money to think about. Someone who works on Saturdays may enjoy the park on a weekday evening instead. Outdoor life is a chance for many people, not a rule for everyone.

1. Why is "every Australian is a beach-loving surfer" a weak picture?

Sample answer

Outdoor life includes bike rides, picnics, walks and sport, not only surfing. These are common activities, not habits of every person, and work, travel time, family and money also shape free time.

Think one step further

Which would you choose: a sunrise swim, a bike ride around a lake, or a picnic in a park? Why?

In your city, what makes outdoor free time easy or difficult?

How are holiday pictures of a country different from real daily life?

Reading

Part 4: Work and free time

Many workers check messages on their phones at night. In Australia, there is now a clear rule about this called the right to disconnect. Employees can refuse to check, read or answer work messages outside their working hours. But there is an important exception: they may have to answer if refusing would be unreasonable.

How do people decide what is unreasonable? Several things matter:

  • Why did the employer make contact?
  • How much does it disturb the worker's free time?
  • Does the worker get extra pay for being available?
  • What are the worker's responsibilities and personal situation?

For example, workers who are on call or doing overtime may still have to respond.

The rule does not stop a manager from sending a message after hours. It gives workers more power to say no in normal cases. Employers and employees are encouraged to talk about what they expect.

A morning swim or a family barbecue needs time that work does not take. Protecting that time is not automatic. It still takes clear conversations and planning.

Check: Part 4: Work and free time

1. A worker is on call and gets an urgent message at night. Can they simply ignore it?

Sample answer

Probably not. Workers who are on call may still have to respond, because refusing could be unreasonable.

Think one step further

Should managers send messages after hours if workers do not have to answer? Why or why not?

A worker gets extra pay to be available on weekends. Is it fair to expect a reply? Explain your reason.

How do you protect your free time from work or study messages?

Reading

Useful Language

Australians use the usual English words too. These informal choices are useful with friends and friendly colleagues. Usage varies, and some expressions are also heard outside Australia.

  • Afternoon → arvo. A short, informal way to talk about the afternoon. “Are you free this arvo?”
  • Breakfast → brekkie. Use it in a casual conversation about food. “Let's have brekkie before work.”
  • Barbecue → barbie. This can mean the meal or the cooking equipment. “We're having a barbie on Sunday. Would you like to come?”
  • No problem → no worries. Use it when something is OK, or to reply to thanks. “Thanks for your help.” “No worries!”
  • Friend / friendly address → mate. You can use it for a friend or when speaking in a friendly way. “Thanks, mate.” It can be used with people of any gender. Listen to the situation first; it may feel too casual in a formal conversation.
  • How are you? → How are you going? This asks how someone is, not how they travel. “How are you going?” “Good, thanks. And you?”
  • Flip-flops → thongs. In Australia, these are light, open shoes for warm weather. “I've packed my thongs for the beach.” The word can mean something different in other countries, so the situation helps you understand it.

What you can do now

Final Reflection

Australia's relaxed image is real in some ways, but it has more behind it than it seems. Friendly talk sits next to serious work, and everyday food carries migration stories. Free time outdoors is common but not automatic, and new rules help people protect it.

  1. Which part of everyday Australia surprised you most, and how is it different from your own daily life?

  2. If you moved to Australia, which habit would you try first: first names at work, a shared barbecue, a weekend outdoor activity, or turning off work messages? Why?

  3. What can a friendly, relaxed public image hide about real work and real effort?
