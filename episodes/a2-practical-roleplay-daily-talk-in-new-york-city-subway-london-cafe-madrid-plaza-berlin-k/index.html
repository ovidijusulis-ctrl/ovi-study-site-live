Reading

Making room on a train

Imagine a short conversation on a New York City subway train. You are standing near another passenger, and your bag is between you.

You want to move without touching the other person. A simple opening is enough: ‘Excuse me.’

Then explain your request: ‘Could you move your bag a little, please?’ The passenger can answer, ‘Of course,’ and move it beside their feet.

You can then stand in the space and say, ‘Thank you.’ If someone asks you to move, answer before you act.

‘Sure, I can move’ tells them you understood. This exchange is about sharing space, not asking for a route or a train time.

Check your understanding

A clear request

1. Why is ‘Could you move your bag a little, please?’ clearer than only saying ‘Excuse me’?

Sample answer

‘Excuse me’ gets the passenger’s attention. The full question explains what you need them to do.

Think one step further

What could you say after the passenger moves the bag?

Useful language

Asking for space

Excuse me.
A polite way to get someone’s attention.Excuse me. Thank you.
Could you move your bag a little, please?
Ask someone to make more space.Could you move your bag a little, please? Thank you.
Sure, I can move
Agree to make space for someone.Sure, I can move. There is enough room now.

Reading

Joining a cafe queue

Picture a conversation in a London cafe. You see people waiting, but you do not know who is last.

Ask a person near the end, ‘Are you waiting in the queue?’ If they say yes, ask, ‘Can I stand behind you?’

Their answer gives you a clear place to wait. You do not need to guess or walk in front of anyone.

If you miss the answer, say, ‘Sorry, could you say that again?’ The other person can repeat it in simple words.

Finish with ‘Thanks’ and join the queue. These questions concern the people beside you; they do not require information about prices or opening hours.

Reading

Before you use something

Imagine sitting beside someone in Madrid’s Plaza Mayor. There is a bag on the next seat, so ask, ‘Is someone sitting here?’

If the person says their friend is using it, answer, ‘No problem,’ and leave the seat free. Now picture an office kitchen in Berlin.

A colleague has a cup beside the sink, and you want to wash your own cup. Ask, ‘Are you still using this space?’

The colleague can finish or make room for you. In both examples, a question is better than moving another person’s things.

Listen to the answer before you act. A polite answer can be yes or no.

Check your understanding

Listening before acting

1. What do the seat conversation and the kitchen conversation have in common?

Sample answer

In both conversations, I ask before using shared space or moving someone’s things. Then I listen to the answer before I act.

Think one step further

How could you answer politely if the space is not free?

Reading

Letting someone pass

Picture a short exchange inside a Paris bakery. You are near the door when another customer wants to leave.

The customer says, ‘Excuse me, could I get past?’ You can answer, ‘Of course,’ and move to the side.

If you also need to leave, ask, ‘Are you going out too?’ A clear answer helps you decide who will move first.

‘Yes, please go first’ makes the next action easy to understand. You can say, ‘Thanks,’ walk through the door, and finish the conversation.

Nobody needs a long explanation about why they are leaving. The important thing is to make your request clear and give the other person room.

Excuse me.

You’re welcome.

A conversation in a shared kitchen

  1. In a shared kitchen, a colleague is using the sink and you want to wash your cup. Speak both parts of a short conversation: get their attention, ask for space, give a clear answer, and finish after they make room.

What you can do now

Final reflection

  1. Clear questions help people share seats, queues, and doorways. A helpful answer tells the other person what they can do next. Which question from these conversations would be useful to you, and where would you use it?
