Cologne Today

Introduction

Imagine a giant church with a crane standing on its tower, not for a few months but for 400 years. That was Cologne for centuries.

The city is also the reason we have the word "cologne" on perfume bottles. Here you will see why the cathedral waited so long, how a perfume took the city's name, and what these two stories have in common.

Before you begin

Warm-up

  1. Can you think of a product that is named after a city or a region?

  2. Have you ever seen a building that stayed unfinished for a long time? What happened?

  3. Why might a city want to be famous for something?

Reading

Part 1: A Cathedral Built to Attract People

Cologne Cathedral stands on a very old religious site. After Roman rule ended, Merovingian kings built a church for the bishop there in the 6th century. So when work on the Gothic cathedral began on 15 August 1248, the builders were not starting from nothing. They were adding a new chapter to a long story.

The people behind the project were the archbishops. They built the cathedral and the city walls to present Cologne as a "Holy" city and as the capital of the land they ruled. A huge church sent a clear message: this place matters.

But a message like that costs money. Much of the early money came from pilgrims who travelled to visit the Shrine of the Three Kings. Think of a popular museum that pays for new rooms with ticket sales. The more visitors came, the higher the stone could rise.

Reading

Part 2: Why the Building Stopped

Work moved fast at first. The eastern part, called the choir, was finished in 1322. Then progress slowed, and finally it stopped. You might guess that the building was simply too hard for medieval workers. In fact, the reasons were mostly about people and money.

First, the city had other needs. In 1474, the craftsmen were needed to make the city's defences stronger against the Duke of Burgundy. A city under threat cares more about walls than towers. Second, the money began to disappear. By 1473, income from pilgrims had fallen. Later, the Reformation and wars made things worse. The cathedral lost its money from indulgences, which were special payments collected by the Church. Major work stopped in 1473 or 1474, and construction ended completely around 1560.

The clearest sign of this pause was on the south tower. It was built only up to the level of the bells, and a large crane stayed on top of it for 400 years. Generations of people in Cologne grew up looking at an unfinished building with a machine on its head.

Check: Part 2: Why the Building Stopped

1. Give two reasons why work on the cathedral stopped.

Sample answer

Craftsmen were needed to strengthen the city's defences against the Duke of Burgundy, and the money from pilgrims and indulgences disappeared.

Think one step further

When danger comes, why might a city choose to spend on walls instead of a cathedral?

Reading

Part 3: Paused, Not Forgotten

The cathedral was never completely forgotten. Efforts to start again began around 1814, and work actually started again in 1823. Even so, proper funding arrived only in the 1840s. Once again, money decided the speed.

The 19th-century builders made an important choice. They completed the main hall (the nave), the side arms (the transepts) and the towers by following the medieval plans. So the finished cathedral is not really a modern building pretending to be old. It is closer to an old idea that waited a very long time for the money to finish it.

This changes the usual question. People often ask why the cathedral took so long. A better question might be: what kept the plan alive while nothing was being built?

Reading

Part 4: An Italian Morning in a German City

Cologne has a second famous symbol, and this one fits in your pocket. In 1709, Johann Maria Farina, an Italian, created a perfume from citrus oils and alcohol. It was inspired by an Italian spring morning after rain. He named it after the city: Eau de Cologne, which means "water from Cologne".

Notice what happened here. The feeling of the perfume came from Italy, but its name came from a German city. So every time someone asked for Eau de Cologne, they also said the city's name.

1. What did Farina use to make Eau de Cologne, and what inspired it?

Sample answer

Reading

Part 5: How a Bottle Carried the City's Name

Famous customers helped the perfume spread. Napoleon, the writer Goethe, Queen Victoria and Empress Elisabeth all used Farina's Eau de Cologne. Napoleon reportedly used two bottles a day. When emperors, queens and great writers choose a product, other people want it too. In this way, Cologne became famous for perfume.

The story has not ended. Farina's company, founded in 1709, is the world's oldest fragrance maker. Family descendants still make the original Eau de Cologne in the city. Its headquarters has been in Farina House since 1723, and today that building is the Fragrance Museum, where visitors can take a 45-minute guided tour.

Now put the two symbols side by side. The cathedral depended on people coming into Cologne. Pilgrims brought money, and when that money stopped, the stones stopped too. The perfume worked the other way. It left the city in bottles and carried Cologne's name to the homes of emperors and queens.

What you can do now

Final Reflection

The cathedral waited for centuries because the people and money that supported it disappeared, not because the idea failed. The perfume moved in the opposite direction and carried the city's name outward. Both stories show that a city's fame depends on what people bring in and what they take away.

  1. What brings visitors into your own city, and what carries its name outward?

  2. Can you think of a project in your country that stopped because of money or politics? Was it ever finished?
