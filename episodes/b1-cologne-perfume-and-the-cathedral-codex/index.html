Introduction

Before you begin

Warm-up

  1. What product do you connect with a particular city or country?

  2. What might make people stop an important building project?

  3. Which tells you more about a city: its famous buildings or its local products? Why?

Reading

Part 1: A City’s Name in a Bottle

The perfume connection begins with a person who came from somewhere else. In 1709, Johann Maria Farina, an Italian, created the original Eau de Cologne. He used citrus oils and alcohol. His inspiration was an Italian spring morning after rain, but he named the perfume after Cologne.

“Eau de Cologne” is French for “water of Cologne.” The name connected the product to the city, although the idea behind its smell came from Italy. A product can therefore belong to a place without every part of its story beginning there.

Famous users included Napoleon and Queen Victoria. Their use helped make Cologne famous for perfume. The city’s name could travel with a bottle, reaching people who had never visited it. That is a different kind of influence from a huge building: small enough to carry, but able to spread widely.

Check your understanding

Check: Part 1: A City’s Name in a Bottle

1. Why does Farina’s perfume connect both Italy and Cologne?

Sample answer

Farina was Italian and took inspiration from Italy, but he created the perfume in Cologne and named it after the city.

Reading

Part 2: A Building Can Work Before It Is Finished

Construction of the Gothic cathedral began in 1248. The project helped promote Cologne as a holy city. Early building work received money from religious visitors who came to the Shrine of the Three Kings.

By 1322, the eastern section was complete and had opened for religious use. This matters because a large church does not have to be completely finished before people can use it.

Imagine a house where some rooms are ready while others still need work. You can live in the finished rooms, although the whole project remains incomplete. The cathedral was a much larger religious project, but the comparison helps explain its history.

Its long building period therefore needs careful reading. The years between starting and finishing do not tell us how many years people actually spent building.

Check: Part 2: A Building Can Work Before It Is Finished

1. Why could the cathedral serve a purpose while construction continued?

Sample answer

Its eastern section was complete and open for religious use by 1322, although other sections remained unfinished.

Reading

Part 3: Why the Work Stopped

Work stopped in stages between 1473 and 1560. There was no single problem that explains the whole pause.

Money from religious visitors declined, weakening an important source of support. In 1474, skilled workers were needed to strengthen the city’s defenses against the Duke of Burgundy. Protecting the city became a more urgent need than continuing the cathedral.

Later, religious changes during the Reformation reduced another source of church income. Wars also affected support for construction.

These pressures worked together. A building project needs money to pay workers, and workers must be available. When either is missing, even a powerful religious purpose cannot keep construction moving.

A large crane remained on the unfinished south tower for about 400 years. It was a visible sign of a project that people had started but could not complete.

Check: Part 3: Why the Work Stopped

1. What two basic needs help explain the pause?

Sample answer

Construction needed both money and available skilled workers, and changing conditions reduced both.

Reading

Part 4: Old Plans Needed New Support

Efforts to restart construction began around 1814, and work resumed in 1823. However, proper funding arrived only in the 1840s. Deciding to continue was one step; finding enough money was another.

The remaining main sections and towers were completed during the nineteenth century using medieval plans. The old design provided direction, but a later generation supplied the support to finish it.

Meanwhile, Farina’s original Eau de Cologne is still made in Cologne by his family’s descendants. Here, continuity means passing a product and business between generations.

The two stories connect through time, but in different ways. The perfume continued through a family business. The cathedral crossed a long break through saved plans and renewed support. Both show how later generations can carry an earlier idea forward.

What you can do now

Final Reflection

Cologne’s fame grew through both a building and a bottle. Their histories show that ideas last when people find ways to support and continue them.
