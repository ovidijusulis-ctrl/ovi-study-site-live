Reading

Part 1: First Nations continuity and Country

Australian culture begins with Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples, also called First Nations peoples. Their cultures are among the oldest continuing cultures on Earth. People have lived on this land for tens of thousands of years, long before British ships arrived.

One key word is Country. In the First Nations sense, Country is a living relationship with land, water, and the wider living world. It is not only a place on a map. Welcome to Country and Acknowledgement of Country ceremonies show that this idea still shapes public life in cities such as Sydney and Melbourne. Torres Strait Islander peoples also keep strong sea and island links across the Torres Strait, north of Queensland.

Reading

Part 2: Convicts, settlers, and the fair go

In 1788 the First Fleet arrived in Sydney, New South Wales. Eleven British ships brought convicts, officers, and supplies, and British colonisation and settler society grew from that landing. Convict transportation and later free settlement changed land, law, and daily life across the continent.

From that history came strong talk about fairness. The fair go means people should get a fair chance. Next to it sits tall poppy syndrome: the habit of cutting down someone who seems too proud or above others. Together, these ideas push talk toward equal treatment, first names, and less ceremony than many visitors expect.

Part 3: Distance, the bush, and life outdoors

Australia is a huge country, and towns can sit far apart. The bush means land away from easy city comfort — dry country, long roads, and small communities. Distance shaped practical habits: people plan travel carefully, look after neighbours, and talk often about weather, roads, and outdoor work.

On the coast, city life looks different but still outdoor. In Sydney and Melbourne, beach time, parks, barbecues, and weekend sport fill ordinary conversation. Cricket, Australian rules football, and rugby league are common shared topics. Distance and outdoor life both push people outside the house and into shared public space.

Part 4: Immigration waves and multicultural cities

After World War II, large immigration waves changed Australian cities. The White Australia policy, which had limited non-European migration, was dismantled across the 1960s and 1970s. From the 1970s onward, official multicultural policy grew, and new arrivals came from Europe, Asia, the Middle East, Africa, and the Pacific in large numbers.

Sydney and Melbourne became clear multicultural centres. In daily city life, visitors hear many languages and see restaurants, shops, and festivals from many family stories. Food streets, suburban temples and churches, and weekend markets show how immigration remade ordinary Australian city culture.

Part 5: Mateship, friendly manners, and direct talk

Mateship is a famous Australian idea of loyal friendship and support between equals. It grew in bush work, wartime service, and everyday help between friends. The friendly word mate still appears in shops, workplaces, and street talk.

Because mateship values equals, manners often feel informal to visitors. People may use first names quickly and get to the point in ordinary talk. Direct talk usually means saying the main idea without long ceremony. Visitors who expect more formal openings can find Australian talk sudden at first, then clear and warm once the friendship pattern shows.

Check: Part 5: Mateship, friendly manners, and direct talk

1. What is mateship, and how does it show up in daily manners?

Sample answer

Mateship is loyal friendship and support between equals, and it shows up in informal manners, helping one another, and friendly direct talk such as using the word mate.

Think one step further

Do you use a friendly word like mate with people you do not know well?

What manners feel informal in your daily life?

Why do you think direct talk can feel friendly to some people and rude to others?

Reading

Part 6: Shared stories visitors notice

Anzac Day is on 25 April. It remembers the Australian and New Zealand Army Corps, or ANZAC, who landed at Gallipoli in 1915, and later service by Australian forces. Dawn services at war memorials, marches in towns and cities, and quiet morning gatherings give the day a shared national shape.

Visitors also notice brighter public signs: the Sydney Opera House, Bondi Beach, and big sport weekends. Those famous pictures travel well in films and tourism. Anzac Day memory gives a more serious shared story beside them, so people learning Australian culture meet both outdoor energy and national remembrance.
