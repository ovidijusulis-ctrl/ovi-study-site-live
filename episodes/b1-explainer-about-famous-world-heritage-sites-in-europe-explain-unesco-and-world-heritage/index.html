  1. Do you know any place in your own country that many tourists travel a long way to see?

  2. What do you think makes an old building worth keeping, when a new building would be cheaper?

  3. Why do some people feel strong emotions when they stand in a very old place?

Reading

Part 1: What UNESCO World Heritage means

Let us start with the word UNESCO. UNESCO is a part of the United Nations. The full name is the United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization, and its office is in Paris. It works on education, science, and culture for the world.

In 1972, countries agreed on a plan called the World Heritage Convention. The idea is simple. Some places are so important that they belong to everyone, not only to one country. UNESCO calls this outstanding universal value. When a place has this value, it can be added to the World Heritage List and becomes a World Heritage Site.

There are three kinds of sites. Cultural sites are made by people, like temples and old towns. Natural sites are made by nature, like mountains and forests. Mixed sites are both. All five places here are cultural sites. These famous World Heritage sites in Europe show why UNESCO protects special places. For each one, we will ask the same three things: who made it, what it was for, and why it still matters today.

Part 2: The Colosseum in Rome

Our first site is in Rome, the capital of Italy. The Colosseum is an amphitheatre, a big round building with seats all around an open space in the middle. It is a very large ancient amphitheatre, and it stands in the centre of the old city.

Building started under Vespasian, the Roman emperor who ruled from 69 to 79 CE, around the year 70 to 72 CE, and it was finished under his son Titus, who opened it in 80 CE. Roman crowds came here to watch gladiator fights, animal hunts, and other big public shows, and tens of thousands of people could sit inside. Earthquakes and stone robbers damaged it over many centuries, but a large part still stands. The Colosseum is protected as part of the World Heritage Site called the Historic Centre of Rome, and it is a place many visitors come to see in Italy.

Part 3: The Acropolis in Athens

The second site is in Athens, the capital of Greece. The word Acropolis means high city. It is a rocky hill that rises above the modern city, and on top of it stand famous temples from classical Athens.

The buildings we see today come mostly from the 5th century BCE, the great time of classical Athens, when the city ran a large building programme after wars with Persia. The main temple is the Parthenon, built for the goddess Athena, who gave the city its name, and other buildings on the hill include the Erechtheion and the Propylaea, the grand gate at the entrance. The Acropolis is a symbol of ancient Greek culture, art, and early ideas of public life in the city. UNESCO added the Acropolis of Athens to the World Heritage List in 1987.

Part 4: Stonehenge in England

Now we go far back in time, to a place with no writing at all. Stonehenge stands in open fields in Wiltshire, in the south of England. It is a circle of huge standing stones, some with other stones lying across the top.

There is no written record from the builders, so we learn from the stones and the ground. Stonehenge was built in several stages over a very long time: the first monument, a large round ditch and bank, was made around 3000 BCE in the Neolithic period, and the big stone circle was put up around 2500 BCE, with work continuing into the Bronze Age for more than a thousand years in total. The stones line up with the sunrise on the longest day of the year and the sunset on the shortest day, and many people were buried nearby, so experts connect it with ceremonies and the dead. English Heritage cares for the site today. Stonehenge is part of a World Heritage Site together with Avebury, another stone landscape nearby, and people still travel from many countries to stand among the stones.

Part 5: The Palace of Versailles in France

Our fourth site is near Paris, in France. The Palace of Versailles is a huge royal palace with long gardens, fountains, and carefully planned outdoor spaces. For a long time it was the centre of French royal power.

The palace grew over time, but the biggest change came in the 17th century under Louis XIV, the French king often called the Sun King, who turned a hunting lodge into a palace that showed the power of the king. The king and his court lived and worked here. Inside, the Hall of Mirrors is a long gallery with many mirrors and windows that face the gardens, and outside the gardens show order, art, and control of nature. Versailles helps people understand royal life, art, and politics in early modern Europe. UNESCO added the Palace and Park of Versailles to the World Heritage List in 1979, and millions of visitors walk through the halls and gardens every year.

Part 6: The Historic Centre of Prague

Our last site is in Prague, the capital of Czechia, also called the Czech Republic. The Historic Centre of Prague is not one building. It is a whole old city centre with streets, squares, bridges, and castles that grew over many centuries.

Builders, traders, kings, and ordinary city people shaped Prague from the Middle Ages onward. The Old Town grew around markets and churches, Prague Castle sits above the river as a long seat of power, and the Charles Bridge crosses the Vltava River and links the old sides of the city. The historic centre was a living city where people traded, prayed, ruled, and walked the same streets that visitors walk today. Prague keeps many layers of European architecture in one walkable place, from medieval lanes to later grand buildings. UNESCO added the Historic Centre of Prague to the World Heritage List in 1992. Together with the Colosseum, the Acropolis, Stonehenge, and Versailles, it shows that World Heritage can protect one famous building or a whole historic city.
