Reading

Part 1: Ancient names and the origins of the Finns

One way to approach this history is to ask: what written records exist, where did the people come from, and how did names and languages change over time?

The first known written mention of the Fenni comes from the Roman writer Tacitus in 98 CE. However, his description of northern hunter-gatherers does not prove that they were the ancestors of modern Finns. Even the origins of the names Finland and Suomi remain uncertain.

Archaeology shows continuous settlement in Finland since about 9000 BCE, but languages and people also moved over time. Ancestors of Finnish speakers came west in several waves. Finnish developed from Proto-Finnic, probably spoken south of the Gulf of Finland. It belongs to the Uralic family and is closely related to Estonian and Karelian—not to Indo-European languages such as Lithuanian. Sámi languages were once spoken across much more of Finland.

Check your understanding

Check: Part 1: Ancient names and the origins of the Finns

1. Why does Tacitus’s text not give a complete origin story for modern Finns?

Sample answer

Tacitus used an uncertain Roman label for a northern people, while archaeology and language research show a much longer and more complex history.

Reading

Part 2: Between Sweden and Russia

From the late 13th century, most Finnish lands became part of Sweden. Turku was the main town, and Swedish was used in government, education, and elite life. In the 16th century, bishop Mikael Agricola created the first comprehensive Finnish writing system. His Abckiria primer appeared in 1543, and his Finnish New Testament in 1551.

Swedish rule ended after the Finnish War of 1808–1809. Finland then became an autonomous Grand Duchy within the Russian Empire. It kept many Swedish laws, while Helsinki became the capital in 1812. Finnish national culture also grew: Elias Lönnrot published the Kalevala in 1835, and an 1863 language decree began raising Finnish toward official status. In 1906, Finland created a one-chamber parliament and became the first European country to give women both the vote and the right to stand for election.

Check: Part 2: Between Sweden and Russia

1. How did the Russian period help Finland develop a separate national system?

Sample answer

Finland kept autonomy and its laws, gained Helsinki as its capital, strengthened the Finnish language, and created a modern parliament.

Reading

Part 3: Independence and a divided country

The Russian Revolution opened a path to independence. Finland’s Senate presented a declaration in December 1917, and Parliament accepted it on 6 December by 100 votes to 88. Soviet Russia recognised Finland soon afterward. Finland still celebrates 6 December as Independence Day.

However, the new country quickly divided. From January to May 1918, the socialist Reds fought the government-supported Whites. The Whites won with help from German troops. The Battle of Tampere was the bloodiest battle, and prison camps caused many later deaths from hunger and disease. Altogether, about 38,000 people died in a population of roughly three million. The conflict left deep political and social wounds. Nevertheless, Finland adopted a republican constitution in 1919, and Kaarlo Juho Ståhlberg became its first president.

Check: Part 3: Independence and a divided country

1. What were the two very different results of Finland’s first years of independence?

Sample answer

Finland created an independent republic, but the Civil War divided society and caused about 38,000 deaths.

Reading

Part 4: War, lost land, and continued independence

The Soviet Union invaded Finland on 30 November 1939, beginning the Winter War. Although Finland had a much smaller army, Finnish forces stopped or defeated Soviet units in places such as Suomussalmi and Raate Road. The war ended in March 1940. Finland remained independent but gave up about nine percent of its land, and more than 422,000 Karelians were evacuated.

Finland fought the Soviet Union again in the Continuation War from 1941 to 1944, this time alongside Germany as a co-belligerent. A major Soviet offensive captured Viipuri in June 1944, but Finnish forces stopped further advances at battles including Tali-Ihantala. The armistice required Finland to give up more territory and then remove German troops from Lapland. Across the two Soviet wars, Finland lost about 12 percent of its land, but it was not occupied and its democratic state survived.

Reading

Part 5: What Finland is today

Modern Finland is a Nordic republic of about 5.6–5.7 million people. Finnish and Swedish are its national languages, while Northern, Inari, and Skolt Sámi have official status in parts of the Sámi homeland. This language map reflects the country’s layered history.

Nature is central to Finland’s image: forests cover most of the land, and official counts include 187,888 lakes larger than 500 square metres. Sauna is another strong cultural symbol. Finland has about 3.2 million saunas, and Finnish sauna culture entered UNESCO’s intangible heritage list in 2020.

Finland joined the European Union in 1995 and adopted euro cash in 2002. After Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022, Finland changed its security policy and applied to NATO. It became NATO’s 31st member on 4 April 2023. Modern Finland therefore combines local traditions, Nordic democracy, European cooperation, and new security choices.
