Before you begin

Warm-up

  1. What do you think makes people share a video with friends?

  2. What kind of meme picture do you think is easy to understand?

  3. What would you do if a friend invited you to join an online movement?

Reading

Part 1: Understand What Spreads

Virality means that content reaches many people in a short time. The content might be an idea, image, video, or written post. An internet meme is a cultural item that people copy, change, and share online.

Use three words to study viral content: hook, handoff, and change. The hook catches attention quickly. The handoff gives someone a simple reason and method to share. Change lets people add their own version. This last step matters because a meme often grows through participation, not only through watching. Your first question should be: “Can someone understand the main idea in a few seconds?”

Reading

Part 2: Give People a Reason to Share

Researcher Jonah Berger described six factors called STEPPS: social currency, triggers, emotion, public, practical value, and stories. In simple terms, people may share something because it makes them look interesting, reminds them of something familiar, creates a feeling, is easy to see, is useful, or tells a story.

Psy released Gangnam Style and its music video on 15 July 2012. The video went viral in August, and on 21 December it became the first YouTube video to reach one billion views. Its strong visual style and memorable performance help us see why public, emotional content can attract attention. When planning, choose one or two strong reasons to share instead of using every factor.

Reading

Part 3: Build a Copyable Format

A viral format needs clear rules, but it also needs space for personal change. In early February 2013, thousands of Harlem Shake videos used a clip from Baauer’s song. A typical video showed one dancer and then used a jump cut to show a wild group dance after the bass drop. People could recognize the structure and create new versions.

The Distracted Boyfriend meme worked similarly with a picture. Antonio Guillem took the stock photograph in 2015. Social media users began using it as a meme in 2017, and it went viral that August. Users added different labels to the same visual situation. For your format, keep one recognizable pattern and one part that people can easily replace.

Reading

Part 4: Turn Sharing into Participation

A movement spreads when people can perform a clear action, show that action publicly, and invite the next person. In 2014, the Ice Bucket Challenge became a viral movement. People poured ice water on themselves, posted a video, and asked others to do the challenge or donate. It raised money and awareness for ALS and motor neurone disease.

This example combined a visible action, a serious purpose, and a direct invitation. If you create a movement, explain the goal and the next step clearly. Make participation safe, honest, and possible for different people. Do not pressure people or hide who benefits. Fast sharing is valuable only when the message and action are responsible.

Reading

Part 5: Test the Handoff

Before a public launch, test your idea with a small group. First, show it without a long explanation. Can people understand the hook? Next, ask them to share or remake it. Can they complete the handoff easily? Finally, compare their versions. Is the main idea still clear after the change?

Viral marketing also uses social networks so people pass a message to others. In 1996 and 1997, Hotmail added a simple tagline to outgoing email and grew very fast. Each message carried the invitation forward. You can use the same principle without copying the campaign: place a short, natural invitation inside the content. Then improve confusing steps and watch meaningful signals such as sharing, remaking, or participation—not only likes.
