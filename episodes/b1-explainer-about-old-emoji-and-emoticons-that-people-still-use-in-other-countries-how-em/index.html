Reading

Part 1: Old Emoticons Are Still Alive

Before emoji became common, people made faces from keyboard symbols. These are called emoticons. A face such as :-) is read sideways, while XD shows strong laughter. They became popular in early email, forums, and chat rooms. They may look old now, but they have not disappeared. Emoticons that people still use in other countries include the Russian closing bracket, XD in gaming chats, and <3 in nostalgic messages.

Some habits developed in particular online communities. In Russian-language messages, a person may write one closing bracket, ), to show friendliness. Several brackets, ))), can suggest stronger warmth or laughter. In other countries, XD and <3 are still used in gaming communities or nostalgic posts. An old face can feel playful, sincere, or unfashionable, depending on who sends it.

Part 2: Emoji Meanings Can Travel and Change

Modern emoji grew from Japanese mobile communication. NTT DoCoMo released an influential set in 1999, and international Unicode support later helped emoji spread across devices and countries. However, the pictures travelled more easily than their original meanings.

For example, 🙏 can express thanks, a request, or an apology in Japan. Elsewhere, people may understand it mainly as prayer, and some users call it a high five. The face 😤 also shows change. Its steam originally suggested triumph or determination in a Japanese visual tradition, but many international users now read it as anger or frustration. Meaning therefore comes from both the picture’s source and the new setting where people use it.

Part 3: Text Faces Look in Different Directions

A kaomoji is a text face that is usually read upright, not sideways. Examples include (^_^) for happiness, (T_T) for crying, and (¬_¬) for doubt or annoyance. These faces became strongly connected with Japanese online culture, although people now use them internationally.

Their design also shows an interesting contrast. In many Western emoticons, the mouth carries the main emotion: compare :-) with :-(. In kaomoji, the eyes often do more of the work. The rest of the face can add arms, tears, or movement. The international shrug is a small text picture made of bars, a round mouth, and raised arms. It carries a simple message: “I do not know” or “What can you do?”

Check: Part 3: Text Faces Look in Different Directions

1. What visual difference often separates kaomoji from older Western emoticons?

Sample answer

Kaomoji are usually upright and often emphasize the eyes, while older Western emoticons are commonly sideways and emphasize the mouth.

Reading

Part 4: Stickers Create Local Chat Habits

Stickers are larger and more detailed than ordinary emoji. They may show a full character, include words, or move through animation. Because apps sell or provide different sticker collections, local messaging habits can develop around particular characters.

LINE stickers are especially familiar in Japan, Thailand, and Taiwan. LINE characters such as Brown and Cony began as messaging stickers and later appeared on many physical products. In South Korea, KakaoTalk has its own popular character stickers, while WeChat users in China often exchange animated images and user-made sticker collections. A sticker can soften a refusal, show thanks, or end a conversation warmly. However, someone outside that app community may not recognize the character or understand the social tone.

1. Why are sticker meanings often connected to particular places or communities?

Sample answer

Reading

Part 5: Read the Person, Platform, and Situation

There is no perfect international emoji dictionary. A useful method is to consider three things: the person, the platform, and the situation. First, age and relationship matter. A simple 🙂 may seem pleasantly polite to one person but cold or passive-aggressive to another. Second, platforms sometimes display the same emoji with slightly different designs. A small change in the eyes or mouth can change its emotional effect.

Finally, online communities create new meanings. In many English-language social media spaces, 💀 can mean “That was so funny I died,” rather than actual danger or death. Old faces can also return as jokes or signs of nostalgia. When meaning is unclear, the surrounding message and the sender’s usual style are often more useful than the symbol alone.
