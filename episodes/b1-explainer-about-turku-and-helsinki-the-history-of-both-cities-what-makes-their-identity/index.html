Part 1: Two Cities, Two Roles

Finland has many towns, but two cities tell a big part of its story. The first is Turku, on the southwest coast. The second is Helsinki, on the south coast. Here, "Turku" means the city in Finland, not the country Turkey. The names only sound a little alike.

Turku is the older city. For hundreds of years, when Finland was part of the Swedish kingdom, Turku was the most important city in the land. Helsinki is younger. It became the capital in 1812, and in 1917 it became the capital of independent Finland.

Some countries in Asia have a similar pattern, with an old capital full of history and a newer capital full of government offices. To understand each city, it helps to look at three things: where it started, the big turning points in its past, and what people feel about it today.

Reading

Part 2: Turku's History — Church, Fire, and a Big Move

Turku's story begins with the church. In 1229, a letter from Pope Gregory IX moved the seat of the local bishop to the area around Turku. Many people count this as the start of the city. The bishop's great church, Turku Cathedral, was probably consecrated on 17 June 1300. "Consecrated" means it was officially made a holy place. In Swedish, the city is called Åbo, and both names are still used.

In 1809, Sweden lost Finland to Russia. For a short time, Turku was the capital of the new Grand Duchy of Finland. Then in 1812, Emperor Alexander I moved the capital to Helsinki, because Turku was too close to Sweden.

In 1827 the Great Fire of Turku began on 4 September and destroyed about 75 percent of the city. The Great Fire of Turku left 11,000 people homeless, and 27 deaths were recorded. The fire damaged Turku Cathedral and the main building of the Imperial Academy of Turku, also called the Royal Academy of Turku, which had been founded in 1640. After the fire, the Academy moved to Helsinki. Today it is the University of Helsinki. The architect Carl Ludvig Engel then drew a new plan for Turku with wider streets.

Part 3: What Makes Turku, Turku

The heart of Turku is the Aura River. The river runs through the middle of the city and meets the sea on the southwest coast. The City of Turku calls the river and its waterfront the city's most attractive place. People walk, meet friends, and sit by the water there. Some say the river is Turku's living room.

Two old buildings stand like bookends along the river. At one end is Turku Cathedral, more than 700 years old. Near the river mouth is Turku Castle, which dates from the 13th century and is one of Finland's most important heritage sites and museums. The area between them is called the Cultural Waterfront.

Turku is also officially bilingual, which means Finnish and Swedish are both official languages. This comes from its long Swedish past. Finally, Turku is a gateway to the sea. Ferries leave from Turku for the Åland Islands and Stockholm, and the city sits next to the Archipelago Sea, which has thousands of small islands.

Part 4: Helsinki's History — From Trading Town to Capital

Helsinki also began under Swedish rule. In 1550, King Gustav Vasa founded Helsinki as a trading town. He wanted it to compete with the strong Hanseatic merchants, who controlled much of the trade on the Baltic Sea. For a long time, Helsinki stayed fairly small.

In 1748, Sweden began building a sea fortress on islands just outside the harbour. It was called Sveaborg. In 1808, the fortress surrendered to Russia, and from 1809 Finland was under Russian rule.

The big turning point came in 1812, when Helsinki became the capital. After that, the city grew quickly, and much of it was planned. A new centre was designed around Senate Square, with the government palace, the main Lutheran church, and the university around it. In 1917, Finland became independent, and Helsinki became the capital of the new country.

Part 5: What Makes Helsinki, Helsinki

Much of Helsinki's identity comes from design and architecture. Senate Square was designed by Carl Ludvig Engel, a German-born architect who had worked in St. Petersburg. His buildings are neoclassical, which means they are white, calm, and ordered, with columns like old Greek temples.

Over a century later, the Finnish architect Alvar Aalto made a very different mark. His Finlandia Hall, a concert and congress hall by Töölö Bay, was finished in 1971. It is covered in white Carrara marble and black granite. It was meant to balance Engel's Senate Square and to celebrate Finnish independence. In 1975, it hosted a famous international meeting that led to the Helsinki Accords. In 2012, Helsinki was named World Design Capital.

Sauna culture and the sea are also part of Helsinki's everyday identity. The sea is half of the city's character. The old fortress of Sveaborg was renamed Suomenlinna, meaning "Castle of Finland," in 1918. It joined the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1991. About 800 people still live on its islands today.

1. How are Senate Square and Finlandia Hall different?

Sample answer

Senate Square is a neoclassical centre designed by Carl Ludvig Engel in the 1800s, when Finland was under Russian rule. Finlandia Hall is a modern building by Alvar Aalto, finished in 1971. It was meant to balance Engel's square and to celebrate Finnish independence.

Reading

Part 6: Two Different Cities, One Shared Story

Today, the two cities feel different. Turku is a riverside city with deep roots. It has Finland's oldest cathedral, a medieval castle, and a strong link to the western islands and Sweden. After losing the capital and the university, it still remained a centre of culture and learning.

Helsinki is the modern capital. It is home to the government and the University of Helsinki. It hosted the 1952 Olympics and was European Capital of Culture in 2000. Its planned centre, its design, and its sea fortress tell the story of a young nation.

The two cities are also connected. Both began under Swedish rule, both were shaped by Russia after 1809, and the same architect, Carl Ludvig Engel, planned new streets in both. Turku shows where Finland came from. Helsinki shows where Finland went next.
