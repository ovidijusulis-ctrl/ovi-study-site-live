Introduction

Before you begin

Warm-up

  1. Where have you seen a red mushroom with white spots — in a book, a game, a film or a forest?

  2. Bright colours on animals or plants are often a warning. Why might nature use colour in this way?

  3. Why do you think some old stories are repeated for hundreds of years, even when nobody can prove they are true?

Reading

Part 1: A Famous Mushroom of European Forests

The fly agaric's scientific name is Amanita muscaria. It has a red cap with white spots and a white stalk. In Britain and Ireland it is widespread, and it grows in woodland and at the edges of woods. You often find it near birch, pine and spruce trees, and this is not an accident.

The mushroom we can see is only a small part of the fungus. It is the fruiting body, a bit like the fruit on a tree. Under the ground there is a large network of thin threads called mycelium. This network connects with tree roots, and the two work as partners. The fungus gives the tree nutrients from the soil, and the tree gives the fungus sugars. So a fly agaric next to a birch tree is part of a team.

The white spots also have a story. When the mushroom is young, a protective layer called a veil covers it. As the cap grows, the veil breaks, and the white spots are pieces that stay behind. Rain can wash them away, so some fly agarics have no spots at all. This is one reason why colour and spots are not a reliable way to judge a mushroom.

1. Why is the mushroom called "fly agaric", and what does the name not prove?

Sample answer

It is called fly agaric because people used it in the past against flies and other insects. The name does not prove that people ate it.

Think one step further

Part 2: Who Actually Ate It, and Where

You may also hear exciting stories about Viking warriors eating fly agaric before battle. Stories like these spread easily because they are dramatic.

But the well-recorded use comes from Siberia, not from Viking Europe. A good rule is this: if a story is very exciting but has no clear record behind it, treat it as a guess. Modern myths about this mushroom are common, and many of them are more sensational than true.

Part 3: Ibotenic Acid and Muscimol

Why does the fly agaric affect people at all? The answer is chemistry. The two main chemicals are ibotenic acid and muscimol. The main effects on the mind are linked to muscimol.

To understand muscimol, think about how the brain sends messages. Brain cells talk to each other with chemical messages. Each message needs a receptor, a receiving place on a cell, like a lock that accepts only certain keys. One important messenger is called GABA. GABA usually tells brain cells to calm down and send fewer signals, a bit like a brake in a car. Muscimol acts on GABA-A receptors, so it pushes on this braking system.

You might expect a brake to make a person simply sleepy. However, the effects of the fly agaric are stranger than that. A person may feel very sleepy and then suddenly become restless and agitated. These states can change back and forth. People can also become confused, see the world differently, have hallucinations or fall into delirium, a serious state of mental confusion.

There is another danger. The amount of these chemicals is different from one mushroom to another. Because of this, nobody can predict how a person will react. This is a key reason why the fly agaric is treated as poisonous, not as a harmless natural experience.

Reading

Part 4: Is It a "Magic Mushroom"?

People often put the fly agaric in the same group as "magic mushrooms". Usually, "magic mushrooms" means mushrooms that contain psilocybin, mostly from a group called Psilocybe. So is the fly agaric one of them? To answer, we need to compare how they work.

In the body, psilocybin changes into another chemical called psilocin. Psilocin acts on receptors for serotonin, another brain messenger, especially the type called 5-HT2A. Muscimol, as you saw, works through the GABA system instead. Serotonin and GABA are separate signalling systems. So the two mushrooms are like two different keys that open two different locks.

This leads to a clear answer. In a broad sense, both mushrooms are psychoactive, which means they change how the mind works and how people see the world. Because of that shared effect, it makes cultural sense to talk about them together, for example in history or in stories. But it does not make scientific sense to treat them as the same thing. The fly agaric is not a psilocybin mushroom. Its chemicals are different, they act in a different way, and one cannot replace the other.

There is also a practical lesson. You cannot tell mushrooms apart safely just by looking at them. Even experts use much more than appearance, so a picture or a colour is never enough.

1. Why is the fly agaric not a "magic mushroom" in the scientific sense?

Sample answer

It does not contain psilocybin; its main chemical, muscimol, works through the GABA system, while psilocin works through serotonin receptors.

Think one step further

Can you think of other things that people group together because they seem similar, even though they work in very different ways?

Part 5: Gummies, Labels and Modern Risks

Today, the fly agaric is not only a forest mushroom or a fairy-tale picture. It has also become a product. In 2025, the German Federal Institute for Risk Assessment warned about gummies and other food-like products containing muscimol. These were being sold online. Both teenagers and adults were using them for fun, and poison centres were receiving cases. Children are especially at risk, because a gummy looks like an ordinary sweet.

A similar problem appeared in the United States. In 2024, an investigation there tested mushroom gummies. Some products said they contained Amanita or a mix of mushrooms. However, several of them also contained psilocybin and psilocin, which were not on the label. Some people who ate gummies labelled with Amanita needed to be checked in hospital.

Be careful about what this shows. It does not mean that the fly agaric naturally contains psilocybin. It means that some factory-made products did not contain what their labels said. Marketing often uses the word "mushroom" in a loose way, and this can mix up two groups that science keeps separate. As a result, a buyer may not know what they are really eating. This was also one small study in the US, so it does not tell us how common these products are in Europe.

In the US, the food safety authority has also stated that fly agaric and its chemicals are not approved for use in normal food. It reported serious risks of poisoning. This is a US rule, not European law, but the main message applies everywhere. A product is not safe just because it is natural, traditional, sold online or packed in a colourful box. None of these things proves that a product is safe or that it works as medicine.

What you can do now

Final Reflection

The fly agaric belongs firmly to European forests, but the well-recorded tradition of eating it belongs mainly to Siberia, and many exciting European stories remain guesses. Muscimol acts on the brain's GABA system, so the fly agaric works differently from psilocybin "magic mushrooms". Pretty pictures, old stories and modern labels can all hide the same simple fact: it is poisonous.

  1. Which surprised you more — the real history of the fly agaric or the modern problems with gummies and labels? Why?

  2. How could you check whether an exciting story about history is well recorded or just a guess?
