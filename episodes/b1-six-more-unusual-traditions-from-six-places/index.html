Part 1: El Colacho — a jump with a special meaning

El Colacho is a tradition in Castrillo de Murcia, a village near Burgos in Spain. It began in 1620, so it is more than 400 years old. It takes place on the Sunday after Corpus Christi, a Christian celebration. In 2026, that Sunday was 7 June.

The tradition is also called baby jumping. Babies born during the past year lie on mattresses in the street. A man dressed as the devil jumps over them. This man is called El Colacho.

The jump has a special meaning. People believe it takes away bad luck and evil. To understand this tradition, remember that the action and the belief belong together. It is not simply a jump over mattresses.

Part 2: The Battle of the Oranges — a story told together

The Battle of the Oranges is the biggest part of the Historic Carnival of Ivrea. Ivrea is a town in Piedmont, in northern Italy. The event lasts for three days before Carnival ends.

Teams on foot throw oranges at throwers riding in carts. The people throwing on foot are called aranceri. There are nine teams on foot, including the Diavoli and the Scacchi. Visitors who do not throw wear a red hat.

Behind these actions is a story about the people of Ivrea standing up against a cruel ruler. The event remembers that story through a shared activity. Here, the meaning is different from El Colacho. It connects the town's celebration with a story about its people.

Part 3: Busójárás — saying goodbye to winter

Busójárás is a six-day carnival in Mohács, a town in southern Hungary. It happens at the end of winter. In 2026, it ran from 12 to 17 February and ended with a big bonfire, a large outdoor fire.

Men called busós wear scary wooden masks and sheepskin coats. These coats are made from sheep's skins. The men also carry noisemakers, objects that make loud sounds. Masks, warm coats, and noise give this celebration its special character.

The custom says goodbye to winter. The season is therefore part of its meaning, not just its date. In 2009, Busójárás joined UNESCO's list of Intangible Cultural Heritage. This list recognises living traditions, including customs that communities continue to practise.

Part 4: Onbashira — a huge shared task

Onbashira is a festival of Suwa Taisha shrine around Lake Suwa in Nagano, Japan. A shrine is a place connected with religious belief. The festival is over 1,200 years old.

It happens in the years of the Tiger and the Monkey, about once every six years. It was last held in 2022, and the next festival is in 2028. Unlike the other traditions described so far, it is not a yearly event.

People move 16 huge fir trees from the mountains to the shrine. The trees are over 150 years old and about 17 metres tall. Riders ride the logs down steep slopes. The shrine is at the centre of the festival, and moving these enormous trees is its shared task.

Part 5: Tinku — meeting with hope for the harvest

Tinku is an Andean ritual from northern Potosí in Bolivia. A ritual is an activity with a special meaning that people repeat. The best-known Tinku takes place in Macha during the Fiesta de la Cruz. In 2026, it was on 3 and 4 May.

Communities come to the town's main square with music, colourful clothes, and dance. The ritual is a meeting between two groups of the community. People believe it brings a good harvest, meaning a good amount of food gathered from the fields.

Tinku is also a famous dance at carnival in Oruro. Its name therefore connects with both a community ritual and a dance. In Macha, hope for the harvest is central to its meaning.

Part 6: Thaipusam — a journey of religious belief

Thaipusam is a Hindu festival for Lord Murugan, celebrated by Tamil people. Its biggest celebration is at Batu Caves, near Kuala Lumpur in Malaysia. In 2026, Thaipusam was on 1 February. Before the festival, more than 2.5 million visitors were expected at Batu Caves. That was an expectation, not a final visitor count.

Devotees, people strongly committed to their religion, climb 272 colourful steps to the temple in the cave. Many carry a kavadi, a decorated frame, or pots of milk.

Religious belief is central to this festival. The steps, the things people carry, and the temple visit are parts of the celebration for Lord Murugan. Understanding that purpose helps us see more than a large crowd or colourful steps.
