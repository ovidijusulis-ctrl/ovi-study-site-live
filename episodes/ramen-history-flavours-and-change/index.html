From Chinese Roots to Modern Ramen

Quality checked lesson

Published 5 Aug 2026

Part 1: A Dish That Crossed Borders

Ramen has Chinese roots, but historians still debate exactly how it entered Japan. We should not imagine one simple arrival story. We do know that a Chinese restaurant in Asakusa was serving an early form of the dish by 1910.

In Japan, cooks gradually adapted the soup, noodles, and toppings. The wheat noodles often contain kansui, which helps give them a firm, bouncy texture. We can understand this history through three ideas: source, change, and result. The source was Chinese noodle cooking. The change happened as Japanese cooks developed the dish. The result was ramen with many local and restaurant styles.

Part 2: Ramen After the War

Ramen became much more common after the Second World War. In 1945, people sold it in postwar black markets. Around 1950, more flour was available, and people returning to Japan also helped ramen shops and varieties expand.

Ramen suited a changing society because a shop could prepare a filling bowl and serve it quickly. At the same time, different cooks could make different stocks, seasonings, noodles, and toppings. This helped ramen grow without becoming one fixed recipe. Using our framework, postwar life was an important source of change. The result was more shops and more opportunities for local styles to develop.

Part 3: Shoyu and Shio—Simple Names, Many Bowls

Four broad ramen families became especially well known: shoyu, shio, miso, and tonkotsu. These names do not describe every ingredient. A stock may use chicken, pork bones, seafood, or a mixture. The cook then adds a main seasoning.

Shoyu ramen uses soy sauce as its main seasoning, while shio ramen uses salt. However, two shoyu bowls can taste very different because their stocks, noodles, and toppings may differ. This balance helps explain the wide appeal of these families. Customers can recognize the main style, but cooks still have room to create. The name gives a clear identity, while the full bowl offers variety.

Part 4: Miso Ramen and a Memorable Combination

Sapporo is strongly connected with miso ramen, which developed there in the 1950s. Its success was not only about adding miso to soup. The different parts of the bowl were designed to work together.

Classic Sapporo miso ramen uses medium-thick, elastic, crinkled noodles. Their shape helps them hold the miso broth. Vegetables such as bean sprouts and onion are also common. The rich seasoning, noodle texture, and vegetables create a memorable combination. This shows why a flavour family can become widely loved: people experience a complete bowl, not only one seasoning. Here, the source is a local style, the change is careful design, and the result is a strong regional identity.

Part 5: Tonkotsu from Kurume to Hakata

Tonkotsu ramen is built around pork-bone broth. In 1947, cloudy tonkotsu broth was served in Kurume, in Fukuoka Prefecture. This became an important influence on later ramen across Kyushu.

Hakata ramen is another famous Kyushu example. It usually has cloudy pork-bone broth and simple toppings. Customers can also order kaedama, an extra serving of noodles for the remaining soup. This gives diners some control over the meal. Tonkotsu became memorable because its broth gave it a clear identity, while shop practices such as noodle refills made the experience distinctive. Again, one regional change produced results that travelled beyond its original city.

Part 6: Shops, Regions, and New Styles

Ramen kept changing as it spread. Each restaurant could choose its stock, seasoning, noodle thickness, toppings, and local ingredients. These choices helped create today’s regional and shop-level variety.

Cooks also made mixed broths, tsukemen with separate dipping soup, and soupless styles. Ramen received wider attention overseas. Restaurant ramen therefore changed in two directions. Some cooks protected a local identity, while others combined ideas and created something new. In Japan, a bowl connected with a particular city can feel different from a convenient bowl near a station or workplace, even when both belong to the same broad flavour family.

Part 7: Instant Noodles Change Time and Place

Restaurant ramen depends on a cook, a kitchen, and a bowl served soon after preparation. Instant noodles changed this relationship. In 1958, Momofuku Ando invented Chicken Ramen, which Nissin describes as the world’s first instant noodles. In 1971, he developed Cup Noodles, described by Nissin as the first cup-type instant noodles.

These products made noodles much easier to store, prepare, and distribute. Ramen-style food could now be eaten at home, at work, or in other places without a ramen shop. Instant noodles did not replace regional restaurant bowls. Instead, they created another path: less individual shop design, but much greater convenience and reach. The result was a food connected both with local craft and everyday speed.
