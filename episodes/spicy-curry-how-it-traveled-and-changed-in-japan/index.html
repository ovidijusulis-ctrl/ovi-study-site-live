Part 1: One Name, Many Kinds of Food

“Curry” does not describe one fixed recipe. Across South Asia and India, different regions have developed many local dishes using their own ingredients and spice combinations. Later, Britain produced commercial curry powder: a prepared mixture that gave cooks a more regular result. This mixture eventually reached Japan, where cooks changed the sauce to suit rice and local meals.

We can understand this journey through three ideas: source, change, and result. The source is an earlier food tradition. Change happens when cooks meet new ingredients, needs, or tastes. The result is a new style that has its own identity.

Part 2: From South Asia to Britain

British colonial contact with South Asia introduced British people to many spiced dishes. However, those dishes did not form one simple system called curry. They came from different places and cooking traditions. In Britain, commercial curry powder turned several spices into a steady, convenient mixture.

This was an important change. A prepared powder could travel, be stored, and give similar results in different kitchens. At the same time, it reduced a wide range of regional dishes to a more general idea of “curry.” Using our lens, South Asian food traditions were the source, commercial powder was the change, and a portable British curry style was the result.

Part 3: Curry Arrives and Develops in Japan

Commercial curry powder entered Japan in the early Meiji period. Japanese cooks thickened the sauce and served it with rice. Curry then became popular in homes, towns, and school lunches. One theory connects this development with the Japanese Navy. A British Royal Navy curry-flavored stew was adapted into a thick sauce that worked with rice and offered a practical meal containing meat and vegetables. Retired sailors later helped spread this naval style.

Japanese companies also made curry powder easier to obtain. Minejiro Yamazaki produced Japan’s first domestically made curry powder in 1923. In 1930, Higashiya, the company before S&B Foods, launched curry powder for home use.

Part 4: A Spice Blend Is a Team

A curry blend does not depend on chili alone. S&B Foods lists ingredients including turmeric, coriander, fenugreek, cumin, pepper, chili pepper, cinnamon, fennel, ginger, star anise, cloves, nutmeg, cardamom, and herbs. Together, they balance flavor, color, and spiciness.

Different spices make different contributions. Chili pepper can create strong heat, while turmeric gives a noticeable color. Cinnamon, cloves, and cardamom can add warm aromas. The final experience depends on the relationship between all the ingredients, not simply on how much chili is present. This returns us to the idea of change: when cooks adjust one part of the blend, the result can smell, taste, and feel quite different.

Part 5: Why Chili Feels Hot

The heat of chili is not the same as the physical temperature of food. Chili peppers contain a compound called capsaicin. Capsaicin activates a receptor in the body called TRPV1. This receptor also responds to real heat, so the brain can receive a heat-like signal even when the food itself is not unusually hot.

That is why spicy curry can create a burning sensation in the mouth. The experience may continue after you swallow because the body is still responding to capsaicin. In everyday conversation, people often describe curry as “hot,” but this word can mean two things: high physical temperature or strong chili heat. The two sensations can also happen together.

Part 6: The Familiar Japanese Curry Experience

A common Japanese home curry has a thick, rich sauce with ingredients such as onions, carrots, potatoes, and meat, and it is usually served with rice. Curry roux blocks make this style convenient. They contain flour, oil, seasonings, spices, and other ingredients, so they help thicken and flavor the whole pot.

This texture changes the eating experience. A thick sauce covers the rice and vegetables, and each bite combines sauce, staple food, and solid ingredients. The spice blend may provide aroma, color, and heat, but the body of the sauce is also important. Because there is no single South Asian curry standard, “different from curry abroad” can mean many things. The comparison depends on the particular dish.

Part 7: A Local Food with an International History

Japanese curry shows how adaptation can create something both international and local. Its sources include varied South Asian food traditions and British commercial curry powder. Important changes in Japan included a thicker sauce, service with rice, practical meat-and-vegetable meals, and convenient products for home cooking. The result became familiar in homes and school lunches.

This history does not mean that Japanese curry is an incorrect copy. Through these changes, it became a familiar part of home cooking and school lunches in Japan. The source–change–result lens also reminds us not to treat all curry as one dish. When we compare curries, we can ask more precise questions: Which region or recipe is the source? What changed? What sensory or cultural result did that change produce?
